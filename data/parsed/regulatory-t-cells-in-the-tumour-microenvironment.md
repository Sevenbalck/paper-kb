## Regulatory T cells in the tumour microenvironment

Charlotte J. Imianowski 1,2,4 , Qiang Chen 1,2,4 , Creg J. Workman 1,2 &amp; Dario A. A. Vignali 1,2,3

## Abstract

The powerful suppressive capabilities of regulatory T (T reg ) cells and their appreciable contribution to tumour progression make them attractive immunotherapeutic targets. However, their role in systemic immune homeostasis makes it important to find ways to specifically target tumour-infiltrating T reg cells while leaving the wider system unperturbed. It is also unknown whether therapies depleting or disrupting the function of tumour-infiltrating T reg cells will provide the greatest efficacy while limiting immune-related adverse events. In addition, T reg cells share much of their biology with conventional CD4 + T cells, introducing challenges when designing targeted immunotherapies. In this Review, we discuss recent advances in differentiating tumour-infiltrating T reg cells from their systemic and tissue-resident counterparts and understanding how the biology of tumour-infiltrating T reg cells differs from conventional CD4 + T cells. We also discuss how recent technological advances may enable the study of tumour-infiltrating T reg cells in even greater detail, helping to identify new targets for next-generation immunotherapeutic drugs.

## Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy has revolutionized cancer treatment, resulting in striking clinical responses in patients with certain cancer types 1 . The cancer immunotherapies most commonly used in the clinic involve blockade of inhibitory receptors, such as programmed cell death 1 (PD1) and cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated antigen 4 (CTLA4), expressed on cytotoxic T cells and usually associated with a dampened immune response 2 . ICI treatments reinvigorate the antitumour responses of cells expressing these molecules, as they are commonly associated with an exhausted phenotype 3 . Treatments targeting other hallmark inhibitory receptors, notably, lymphocyte activation gene 3 (LAG3; also known as CD223), T cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain 3 (TIM3) and T cell immunoreceptor with immunoglobulin and ITIM domain (TIGIT), are currently being pursued for the development of next-generation and combination therapeutic approaches to improve both efficacy and response 4 . Some of these new combination therapies are clinically promising 5 . Cell-based therapies, for example, chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-expressing T cells, are another arm of clinical cancer immunotherapy 6 . These therapies are efficacious in blood-based cancers (for example, B cell acute lymphoblastic leukaemia), in part due to tumour-specific antigens that do not lead to off-target effects, but also due to the lack of an immunosuppressive tumour microenvironment (TME) in these cancers 7 .

One of the biggest barriers to improving patient responses to cancer immunotherapies in solid tumours is the presence of an immunosuppressive TME, and a major component of this is forkhead box protein P3-positive (FOXP3 + ) regulatory T (T reg ) cells 8-10 . Constituting only 5-15% of the CD4 + T cell population, these powerfully suppressive immune cells are essential to prevent autoimmunity, limit inflammation and maintain local immune homeostasis in tissues 11-14 . However, Treg cells are also recruited to tumours and can mediate peripheral tolerance 15 . Indeed, favourable prognosis and survival in multiple cancer types in the absence of immunotherapeutic treatment is associated with low T reg to CD8 + T cell ratios 9,16 , indicating that an abundance of T reg cells within the tumour, along with expression of inhibitory receptors, is largely responsible for limiting tumour clearance by CD4 + and CD8 + effector T (T eff ) cells 10,17 . In mice, depletion of T reg cells can promote antitumour immunity and enhance tumour rejection, but at the cost of developing autoimmune side effects 18 . Poor responses to immunotherapies are also associated with high T reg to CD8 + T cell ratios 19,20 .

Treg cells can be either thymically derived (tT reg cells) or peripherally induced (pT reg cells). In general, tT reg cells recognize and restrain responses to self-antigens to maintain systemic tolerance, having developed from CD4 single-positive thymocytes that bear T cell receptors (TCRs) with intermediate-to-high-strength affinity for them 21 . Mature CD4 + FOXP3 conventional T (T conv ) cells upregulate FOXP3 in the presence of transforming growth factor-β (TGFβ) to become pT reg cells 22 . These cells are usually associated with maintaining tolerance to environmental antigens, for example, those from commensal bacteria in the gut and on the skin. T reg cells in human tumour and non-tumour tissues share a similar TCR repertoire, but there is limited overlap with CD4 + T conv cells, suggesting that tumour-infiltrating T reg cells may derive mainly from tTreg cells as opposed to being peripherally induced 23,24 . Although the sources of tumour-infiltrating T reg cells across cancer types in human and mouse have yet to be fully defined, this Review focuses mainly on tT reg cells with a few examples related to pT reg cells.

In the clinic, anti-CD25 therapies have been used to target T reg cells to improve cancer outcomes; however, these therapies are not tumour specific. Owing to their important role in maintaining immune homeostasis and preventing autoimmunity, tumour-infiltrating T reg cells must be selectively targeted for depletion or disruption of function. This is an active area of research that has the potential to unlock a new arm of cancer immunotherapy, which could be combined with existing treatments to improve therapeutic outcomes. Thus far, however, the identification of potential tumour-infiltrating T reg cell markers has been limited. A notable example that has shown some therapeutic promise is CC-chemokine receptor 4 (CCR4). Future treatments will rely on an increased understanding of tumour-infiltrating T reg cell biology and how it differs from both CD4 + and CD8 + T eff cells in the tumour and Treg cells in the periphery, as well as the identification of new markers that distinguish tumour-associated T reg cells, potentially across multiple cancer types, and are viable therapeutic targets with minimal off-target effects or immune-related adverse events 25 .

This  Review  covers  five  main  areas  of  interest  around Treg cells in tumours: (1) new insights into heterogeneity within the tumour-infiltrating T reg cell population; (2) expression of inhibitory receptors by tumour-associated T reg cells and how these influence their function; (3) mechanisms used by T reg cells to mediate their suppressive functions in the TME; (4) mechanisms that drive recruitment and retention of T reg cells into the tumour; and (5) how the metabolism of Treg cells adapts to the TME to provide an advantage over CD4 + and CD8 + Teff cells. We link these areas to current and possible future therapeutic approaches that target tumour-infiltrating T reg cells and discuss which areas show most promise.

## Tumour-infiltrating T reg cell heterogeneity

In multiple cancer types, increased numbers of T reg cells are associated with worse prognosis in terms of both disease progression and patient survival, especially when compared proportionally with CD8 + T cells 26 . This includes ovarian 27-29 , breast 30-33 , lung 34-37 , hepatocellular 38-42 , renal 43 , pancreatic 44 , gastric 45 , cervical 46 and colorectal 47 cancers. Counterintuitively, however, some cancer types with increased numbers of intratumoural T reg cells have better prognosis 16 , including breast 48 , colorectal 49,50 and ovarian 51 cancers. There are several possible reasons for this, and perhaps the foremost is their diverse nature: T reg cells represent a functionally and phenotypically heterogeneous population 52,53 . Identifying markers that define specific T reg cell populations is thus of intense interest.

Analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing data from colon, skin and melanoma to characterize regulatory and memory T cell populations revealed an expression profile associated with a T reg cell subset that is conserved across skin tissue and melanoma tumours in mouse and human 54 . Furthermore, analysis of chromatin accessibility and gene expression indicates similarity between tumour-infiltrating T reg cells and those in healthy tissues 55 . However, other studies have indicated that unique transcriptional and phenotypic characteristics could differentiate tumour-infiltrating T reg cells from those in healthy tissues 56,57 . Further understanding of how tumour-infiltrating T reg cells differ from Treg cells in the periphery or those resident in normal tissues could help to identify new immunotherapeutic targets.

Even within the tumour, the T reg cell population is phenotypically and functionally heterogeneous 58 . Several subpopulations of FOXP3 + Treg cells have been identified based on function: quiescent (sometimes called naive) T reg cells, effector T reg (eT reg ) cells, pro-inflammatory Treg cells and T follicular regulatory (T FR ) cells (Fig. 1). Studies have begun to characterize tumour-infiltrating T reg cells in greater depth, identifying populations with different levels of suppressive function. In general, high levels of the more suppressive eT reg cells are associated with worse prognosis, and studies have sought to identify population-specific markers to segregate eT reg cells from their more quiescent counterparts. Tumour-infiltrating T reg cells could be divided into six distinct clusters, with two clusters associated with interferon (IFN)-response genes and another two clusters with tumour necrosis factor receptor (TNFR) signalling. Use of these signalling pathways was mutually exclusive, suggesting heterogeneity within the population. Of note, in various human cancers, tumour-infiltrating T reg cells that express TNFR superfamily (TNFRSF) genes exhibit an effector-like phenotype and are highly suppressive 61,62 . Interestingly, the transcription factors interferon regulatory factor 4 (IRF4) and basic leucine zipper transcriptional factor ATF-like (BATF), which in homeostatic conditions are required for the formation of non-lymphoid T reg cells and for the regulation of eTreg cell suppressive function, are also critical for activating this highly suppressive T reg cell subset 63-69 .

In another study, sequencing of T reg cells from human non-smallcell lung cancer (NSCLC) and mouse colorectal cancer identified several cell clusters with high concordance across species 70 . In human samples, Treg cells clustered into resting, T helper 1 (T H 1)-like cytotoxic, type I IFN-responsive and activated T reg cells. A subcluster of OX40 high GITR high T reg cells was found to be highly suppressive and enriched in patients who did not respond to anti-PD1 therapy. OX40 (also known as TNFRSF4) and GITR (also known as TNFRSF18) are co-stimulatory receptors within the TNFRSF, which also includes 4-1BB (also known as TNFRSF9), whose expression on tumour-infiltrating Treg cells also marks a highly suppressive subset 58,71 . Although all three of these co-stimulatory receptors are highly expressed on tumour-infiltrating T reg cells and associated with poor immunotherapeutic outcomes, their specific role in T reg cell function remains unknown 72-74 . However, given that co-expression of these markers is highest among tumour-infiltrating T reg cells, simultaneously targeting multiple receptors could localize therapies to the tumour and remove the most suppressive T reg cells from the TME.

An additional layer of complexity is that T reg cells can display a functionally unstable or fragile phenotype 75 . These populations are examples of pro-inflammatory T reg cells. T reg cell instability is defined by downregulation of FOXP3 expression in formerly FOXP3 + T reg cells, thus termed 'exT reg ' cells 76 . These cells take on some phenotypic characteristics of helper T (T H ) cell subsets and can produce pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IFNγ. Fragile T reg cells maintain their FOXP3 expression yet develop the ability to produce IFNγ and thus replace suppressive with pro-inflammatory functions 77 . Mechanistically, T reg cells chronically exposed to the pro-inflammatory cytokine interleukin-12 (IL-12) upregulate IFNγ expression and this is associated with a reduction in FOXP3 levels, thereby IL-12 signalling is responsible for promoting both Treg cell fragility and instability 78 . Conversely, autocrine signalling by TGFβ promotes T reg cell stability by repressing the expression of IL-12 receptor subunit β2 (IL-12RB2) and rendering the cells less sensitive to IL-12 signalling, such that TGFβ-deficient T reg cells produce high levels of IFNγ after stimulation with IL-12 79 . TGFβ also inhibits activation of the AKT-mTOR axis, the increased activity of which is associated with increased IFNγ and TNF production in CD4 + FOXP3 + cells 80 . IFNγ signalling can also promote IFNγ expression in T reg cells using an IL-12-independent mechanism in certain viral contexts 81 . In the TME, IL-23R signalling has been associated with T reg cell instability and the acquisition of a T H 1 cell phenotype 82 . Although most therapeutic strategies seek to deplete T reg cells in the tumour or prevent their entry, consideration should be given to whether and how some therapies alter their function. For example, a therapy that drives T reg cell instability and fragility, especially if specific to the tumour, would be advantageous by not only removing a suppressive population but also reprogramming it into a pro-inflammatory phenotype that could aid the antitumour response of CD4 + and CD8 + T eff cells. Indeed, an agonistic GITR antibody was shown to promote T reg cell instability in a tumour-dependent manner, in which FOXP3 loss was specific to tumour-infiltrating T reg cells and associated with decreased Helios expression and IL-10 production, and increased T-box transcription factor (TBET) and eomesodermin (EOMES) expression and IFNγ production 82 .

As alluded to above, there is evidence in some cancer types that increased intratumoural T reg cells are counterintuitively associated with better prognosis 16,83 . Recent work has shed some light on how this may relate to T reg cell heterogeneity, using colorectal cancer as an example 84 . This study divided colorectal cancer cases into two main groups: the first displayed an abundance of eT reg cells with highly suppressive characteristics, whereas the other had more pro-inflammatory CD4 + FOXP3 + cells exhibiting lower FOXP3 expression and increased expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines. In the first group, high T reg cell frequencies positively correlated with poor prognosis, whereas in the second group, favourable prognosis was associated with abundant pro-inflammatory CD4 + FOXP3 + cells. Human CD4 + T conv cells have been shown to transiently upregulate low levels of FOXP3 expression upon stimulation, which is not associated with acquisition of suppressive function, providing a potential caveat to defining these cells as a bona fide T reg cell population without further investigation 85-87 . However, it is clear that understanding the heterogeneity of the T reg cell population within tumours can be key to not only understanding prognosis but also therapeutic success.

Tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs) support the interaction of T follicular helper (T FH ) cells and B cells, thus facilitating antibody production 88 . In humans, tumour-associated TLSs are more prevalent in responders than non-responders to ICI therapy 89-91 . BCL6-expressing TFR cells found in tumour-associated TLSs highly express the surface markers CXC-chemokine receptor 5 (CXCR5) and GITR and are associated with suppression of immune responses 92 . Another study has found that tumour-infiltrating T FR cells are highly prevalent in human cancer and are primarily located in the TLSs 93 . CXCR5 + GITR + T FR cells showed extensive TCR similarity with CXCR5 -CD127 GITR + T reg cells, indicating they, and not T FH cells, give rise to T FR cells in the tumour. Furthermore, in mouse melanoma and colorectal carcinoma tumour models, TFR cells had increased suppressive function and persistence. Adoptive transfer studies have also indicated that T FR cells were better at restricting antitumour immunity than T reg cells. When analysed over time, T FR cells were shown to numerically increase, which suggested ongoing conversion of T reg cells to T FR cells in the tumour, supported by single-cell-trajectory analysis. Further analysis could be conducted to confirm this correlative data, for example, using T reg cell lineage-tracing experiments or analysis of the TCR clonality of expanded T FR populations to see whether these represent outgrown T reg cell clones. Furthermore, the T FR frequency in the mouse melanoma model is relatively low, possibly due to infrequent TLS formation in mouse models compared with human samples. This highlights a need to develop a mouse model with better enrichment of T FR cells in which to study their mechanisms of development and function. Finally, treatment of these models with anti-PD1 therapy increases the number of T FR cells, indicating a possible mechanism for ICI therapy failure. Another study associated B lymphocyte-induced maturation protein 1-positive (BLIMP1 + ) T FR cells with increased risk of melanoma metastasis 94 . However, how T FR cells

Given their important role in maintaining immune homeostasis, Treg cell-targeted immunotherapy must be tumour specific. In addition, Treg cells within the tumour exhibit heterogeneity, so targeting T reg cells exhibiting the most suppressive function may be sufficient to enhance antitumour immunity. Studies are currently focused on identifying these subpopulations and their distinguishing features, including expression of key cell-surface markers or novel combinations that can be targeted for depletion. Given recent advances in sequencing techniques, including the rapid development of spatial sequencing approaches, it is feasible that unidentified tumour-specific markers for T reg cell populations may be found and will show clinical benefit when targeted. Most markers of highly suppressive tumour-specific Treg cells that have currently been identified belong to the TNFRSF, and a limitation of developing depleting antibodies targeting these molecules would be that they would also deplete activated CD8 + T cells and Tconv cells, which upregulate these molecules. Indeed, at present, antibodies targeting these molecules being tested for efficacy in the clinic are intended as agonist therapy for this reason. However, because Treg cells exhibit functional plasticity, therapies that disrupt their function or instigate T reg cell instability or fragility could be equally effective. In fact, there are already preclinical examples of agonist therapies having destabilizing effects on T reg cells while promoting the function of CD8 + T cells, such as a bispecific OX40-4-1BB targeting antibody 95 . Further mechanistic insights into why this is the case may aid future therapeutic design.

## Inhibitory receptor expression

Treg cells express high levels of inhibitory checkpoint molecules, particularly in the TME 96 . Traditionally, ICI therapies seek to block the inhibitory mechanisms that result from expression of checkpoint molecules on effector CD4 + and CD8 + T cells and thus reinvigorate the immune response. However, as these molecules are also expressed on T reg cells, consideration should be given to how these cells respond to therapy. Expression of some checkpoint molecules (for example, CTLA4, LAG3 and TIGIT) are directly associated with how T reg cells mediate their suppressive functions 97 . Others (for example, PD1 and TIM3) are highly expressed by T reg cells and are important for their regulatory functions, although the mechanisms are currently unknown 98,99 . Here we discuss the expression patterns of several inhibitory receptors by T reg cells and how this may affect their function in tumours.

## CTLA4

Treg cells constitutively express the checkpoint molecule CTLA4. Mice deficient for CTLA4 exhibit profound autoimmunity and inflammatory disease, which is replicated in mice with T reg cell-specific deletion of CTLA4, indicating its importance in mediating suppression by Treg cells 100-102 . CTLA4 may also modulate T reg cell fragility in certain metabolic environments, and the effect may be dependent on co-stimulatory CD28 signalling in T reg cells and the resulting increase in their glycolytic rate 103 . By utilizing a glycolysis -defective variant of the 4T1 mouse mammary carcinoma line, enhanced CD8 + T cell-mediated antitumour immunity was observed as a direct consequence of increased expression of IFNγ and TNF by T reg cells, and this was dependent on the availability of glucose in the TME. This indicates enhanced fragility in FOXP3 + T reg cells, although the relative contribution of exT reg cells was not assessed. Further studies could use FOXP3-Cre-inducible lineage tracing to determine whether the increased IFNγ and TNF expression in the CD4 + FOXP3 population comes from exT reg cells rather than from Tconv cells, and whether anti-CTLA4 treatment in this context is also enhancing lineage instability.

Treatment with anti-CTLA4 acts directly on T eff cells to augment CD28 co-stimulation and can block CTLA4-mediated immunosuppression by T reg cells. In addition, some anti-CTLA4 therapies can deplete Treg cells via Fc receptor-mediated antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) and antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP) 25 . Relative depletion of T reg cells is dependent on the Fc isotype of the IgG of anti-CTLA4, thus not all anti-CTLA4 therapies deplete T reg cells equally 104 . Counterproductively, however, CTLA4 therapy can also promote Treg cell expansion, necessitating combination therapy 105 . There is still some debate not only over the relative contributions of different outcomes of anti-CTLA4 therapy to antitumour immunity but also whether other populations of CTLA4 high cells are depleted by anti-CTLA4 therapy, given the upregulation of CTLA4 by CD8 + T cells in tumours 25,96,106 . Indeed, it has been shown that Fc modification to enhance ADCC and ADCP of a CTLA4 antibody could result in selective depletion of T reg cells and expansion of antigen-specific CD8 + T cells if antibody treatment was given before CD8 + T cell stimulation using a cancer antigen vaccine, thus delaying CTLA4 upregulation and sparing these cells from depletion 107 . Thus, genetic engineering of existing therapies could improve specificity and tumour outcomes.

## LAG3

Activated T reg cells express the checkpoint molecule LAG3, which is required for their proliferation and suppressive activity 108 . High LAG3 expression on T reg cells is observed in patients with melanoma, colorectal carcinoma, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma and NSCLC 109-111 . LAG3 + T reg cells possess an activated and suppressive phenotype, producing high levels of immunosuppressive cytokines 109 .

## TIGIT

The co-inhibitory molecule TIGIT is highly expressed by a subset of mouse Treg cells and most human T reg cells but is further upregulated in the TME and associated with poor clinical outcomes upon ICI treatment 112,113 . TIGIT expression also enhances T reg cell immunosuppressive function and stability 114 . TIGIT + T reg cells are more activated, upregulate more suppressive gene signatures than TIGIT -T reg cells and are more suppressive regardless of whether they are from the periphery or tumour 112,113,115 . TIGIT expression by tumour-infiltrating Treg cells is higher than in peripheral T reg cells or T eff cells. Accordingly, anti-TIGIT treatment can selectively deplete tumour-infiltrating Treg cells in mice, whereas in vitro assays using human peripheral blood mononuclear cells from healthy donors compared with patients with cancer have shown that culturing with TIGIT antibodies preferentially resulted in depletion of T reg cells while leaving CD4 + and CD8 + T eff cells unperturbed 116 .

## PD1

PD1 is highly expressed on tumour-infiltrating T reg cells compared with splenic T reg cells 117 . Treatment with anti-PD1 increases T reg cell activation, showing that PD1 has an important role in restraining T reg cell function 118,119 . In addition, examination of tumour-infiltrating T reg cells in the presence and absence of PD1 blockade has shown that anti-PD1 therapy increases T reg cell proliferation and protection from apoptosis 119 . Mechanistically, PD1 signalling promotes PI3K-AKT signalling, which restrains T reg cell suppressive functions, as shown through analysis of the functionality of PD1-deficient T reg cells 120 . Clinically, use of a PI3Kδ inhibitor resulted in improved tumour responses due to systemic depletion of Treg cells, further indicating the importance of PI3K signalling for T reg cell homeostasis 125 .

Furthermore, anti-PD1 therapy can promote T reg cell accumulation in the tumour through a T reg cell-extrinsic pathway dependent on IL-2 produced by activated CD8 + T cells 126 . In addition, anti-PD1 therapy can increase the proportion of highly suppressive T FR cells in the tumour 93 . Similarly, mice deficient in PD1 and PDL1 expression have higher quantities of T FR cells with increased suppressive capacity than T FH cells 127 . Recent studies have also shown that the level of PD1 expression on eTreg cells in tumours can affect the efficacy of PD1 blockade therapy. Co-culture suppression assays using PD1 high T reg cells with PD1 low CD8 + T cells from tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes of NSCLC treated with PD1 blockade exhibited increased Treg cell-mediated suppression, indicating that anti-PD1 therapy could have the unwanted effect of enhancing Treg cell suppressive function in tumours, highlighting the potential risk of therapies targeting molecules expressed both on T eff and Treg cells 128 . Considering not only the cellular makeup of the TME but also the relative expression of checkpoint molecules on T eff and Treg cell populations could indicate potential efficacy of checkpoint therapy in different patients 129 .

However, the effect of PD1 on T reg cell function is somewhat controversial, as some studies have shown that PD1 high T reg cells display a dysfunctional phenotype and increased IFNγ expression, which is enhanced in tumour-infiltrating T reg cells by PD1 blockade in humans 130 . Similarly, in mice, anti-PD1 treatment resulted in enhanced IFNγ expression by T reg cells, indicating that T reg cell fragility can be induced using this therapy 131 . Furthermore, blockade of PD1 or Treg cell-specific deletion of PD1 is associated with lineage instability and a reduced tumour-infiltrating T reg cell pool in a mouse tumour model 117 . Further investigation of different tumour types as well as differences in T reg cell populations in PD1-responsive and PD1-non-responsive patients may provide more clarity. In addition, the immunosuppressive mechanisms affected by PD1 expression in T reg cells have yet to be identified but could help to guide therapeutic strategies.

## Effect of inhibitory receptor blockade and combination strategies on T reg cells

Antibody blockade therapies also may benefit from combination strategies designed to better target tumour-infiltrating T reg cells. For example, a recent study in patients with melanoma has shown that treating first with anti-CTLA4 followed by anti-PD1 results in better survival than either monotherapy or treatment with anti-PD1 followed by anti-CTLA4 93 . Investigating the tumour-infiltrating T FR population revealed that anti-CTLA4 therapy preferentially depletes these cells compared with other tumour-infiltrating T reg cell populations, possibly due to higher expression of CTLA4. Therefore, the suggested mechanism is that treatment with anti-CTLA4 therapy first depletes T FR cells before anti-PD1 therapy and thus avoids potential activation of PD1 +

Treg cells. Accordingly, the combination treatment given in this order increases tumour infiltration by CD8 + T cells. Similarly, dual targeting of CTLA4 and CD47, a molecule that limits phagocytosis, preferentially depleted ICOS high T reg cells and reduced therapeutic toxicity 132 . ICOS (inducible T cell co-stimulator) is a member of the CD28 superfamily, which is implicated in the secretion of IL-10 and TGFβ in T cells 133,134 . ICOS expression correlates positively with the proliferation and suppressive function of eT reg cells in tumours 135-137 . Furthermore, given the important contributions that T reg cells make to the TME and their key interactions with other cell types in the tumour, it is important to understand how their absence may affect sensitivity to ICI therapies to rationally design new therapeutic interventions. For example, investigation of gene expression changes in the TME after the deletion of T reg cells identified upregulation of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and CCR2 signalling as key early changes which occur. Accordingly, combining anti-PD1 treatment with short-term VEGF blockade improved control of anti-PD1 resistant lung adenocarcinoma progression 138 . Finally, as anti-PD1 treatment was associated with promoting Treg cell expansion by inducing IL-2 expression by CD8 + T cells, combining this therapy with previous anti-ICOS treatment enhanced efficacy by disrupting crosstalk between T reg cells and CD8 + T cells 126 .

A key limitation to improving immunotherapeutic outcome is our inadequate understanding of how ICIs affect intratumoural T reg cells. In fact, in many cases, there is conflicting information regarding their precise mechanisms of action, as outlined in the examples above. Other options include improved therapeutic regimens, such as the use of bispecific antibodies that simultaneously target multiple inhibitory receptors to enhance efficacy, or treatment combinations that serve to negate the effect of ICI therapies on T reg cells while allowing T eff cell function to be enhanced. Indeed, some studies outlined in this subsection have proved that such combinations, including the order in which different treatments are given, can feasibly improve therapeutic outcomes. Further mechanistic insights, such as linking differences in T reg cell metabolism to their response to ICI therapy, may guide the design of novel combination therapy strategies to test in the clinic.

## Treg cell-mediated suppression

Treg cells use multiple mechanisms to mediate immunosuppression. Some mechanisms involve direct cell-cell interactions between Treg cells and their targets for suppression (for example, T conv cells or CD8 + T cells), whereas other mechanisms are T reg cell contact independent and act through soluble molecule signalling or through another cell type, such as dendritic cells (DCs). Here we discuss the major suppressive mechanisms used by T reg cells (Fig. 2).

## Inhibitory receptor-associated mechanisms

Perhaps the most well-characterized suppressive mechanism used by Treg cells is regulating access to the co-stimulatory ligands CD80 and CD86 on antigen-presenting cells, including DCs, by Tconv cells through constitutive expression of CTLA4 on the surface of Treg cells 102,139,140 . CTLA4 shares homology with CD28, which allows it to compete for ligand binding (CD80 and CD86) and thus suppresses the co-stimulation required for T conv cell activation 141,142 . CTLA4 expressed by T reg cells cycles between the cell surface and cytosol, and in doing so it captures CD80 and CD86 by transendocytosis and trogocytosis , causing internalization and degradation of these ligands. This renders them unavailable to activate T eff cells, leading to anergy 143,144 . Indeed, a recent study has shown that treatment with the IDO blocker 1-methyl-DL-tryptophan could reduce the efficacy of anti-CTLA4 therapy, indicating an inhibitory interaction between CTLA4 and IDO 148 .

LAG3 expressed on T reg cells exerts its function upon binding to its canonical ligand major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC-II) on DCs, resulting in reduced CD86 expression 149 . Therefore, LAG3 expression on T reg cells, similar to CTLA4, results in reduced co-stimulation of Tconv cells. TIGIT expressed by T reg cells binds to one of its two ligands, CD155, on DCs 114 . This indirectly inhibits T cell function by causing DCs to reduce IL-12 expression and increase IL-10 production, promoting a tolerogenic DC phenotype capable of inhibiting T cell activation 150 . Ligation of TIGIT also leads to the production of IL-10 by T reg cells as well as fibrinogen-like protein 2 (FGL2), expression of which is required for TIGIT + T reg cells to suppress pro-inflammatory responses mediated by TH1 and T H 17 cells 112 .

## IL-2 sequestration

Treg cells constitutively express the high-affinity IL-2 heterotrimeric receptor containing the IL-2Rα chain CD25 (refs. 14,151,152). However, they do not produce IL-2 despite being reliant on it for their development and survival 153,154 . By contrast, resting T eff cells express the lower-affinity IL-2R containing only β-chain (CD122) and γ-chain (CD132) 155,156 . This allows T reg cells to outcompete T eff cells for access to IL-2 (known as IL-2 sequestration) 157-161 . Given the key role of IL-2 signalling in differentiation, expansion and survival of T eff cell populations, sequestration of IL-2 by T reg cells suppresses immune responses by preventing these key biological processes, ultimately leading to T eff cell apoptosis 162-166 .

As T reg cells express high levels of CD25 compared with T conv cells, CD25-targeting therapies have been pursued to deplete T reg cells within the TME 167,168 . This has shown some promise in preclinical studies, with mice displaying smaller tumours and enhanced survival 169,170 . Since its approval by the US Food and Drug Administration, the IL-2R-targeting agent denileukin has been mildly successful in treating renal cell carcinoma and T cell lymphoma but is not effective against other cancer cell types such as melanoma 167,168 . A main limitation of CD25-targeting therapies is that this receptor can be upregulated on activated T eff cells, and anti-CD25 therapies including monoclonal antibodies can block IL-2 signalling important for antitumour activity and deplete T reg cells peripheral to the tumour, leading to mild autoimmune side effects 170 . In short, they are often not specific enough to tumour-associated T reg cells to have no off-target effects. Next-generation CD25 monoclonal antibodies have sought to ameliorate some of these pitfalls by Fc-optimizing the antibodies to enhance binding to activating Fc receptors required for the depletion of T reg cells by ADCC and ADCP while maintaining IL-2-signal transducer and activator of signalling 5 (STAT5) signalling on T eff cells, thus improving antitumour immunity while minimizing toxic side effects 171 .

## Catalysis of immunosuppressive molecules

Other suppressive molecules expressed by Treg cells include the ecto-enzymes CD39 and CD73, which both help to increase inhibition of T conv cells through the second messenger cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP). CD39 functions to convert adenosine triphosphate (ATP) to adenosine diphosphate (ADP) and adenosine monophosphate

(AMP), and CD73 then converts AMP to adenosine 172 . This, in turn, acts through the A 2A adenosine receptor on activated T eff cells to increase intracellular cAMP and thus inhibit their activation, proliferation and expansion 173 .

## Immunosuppressive cytokine expression

Treg cells also mediate suppression in a contact-independent manner by producing immunosuppressive cytokines such as IL-10, IL-35 and TGFβ 10 . IL-10 and IL-35 are expressed by distinct T reg cell populations, and both prevent inflammation in tissues 174-176 . Cell-specific absence of IL-10 expression in T reg cells in mice results in colitis, and this is replicated in mice with T reg cell-specific deletion of BLIMP1, a transcription factor required for the differentiation of IL-10 + T reg cells 65,177 . This reflects the importance of IL-10 in preventing colitis specifically as opposed to systemic inflammation 177 . IL-35 restricts proliferation and memory cell formation and promotes an exhausted phenotype within the CD8 + T cell population 178,179 . Although they share transcriptional profiles and a certain amount of plasticity, IL-10 + and IL-35 + T reg cells represent two distinct subpopulations in the tumour, capable of inducing an exhausted phenotype in CD8 + T cells in a non-redundant manner by instigating upregulation of BLIMP1 (refs. 174,175). TGFβ exerts its function on T H cells by inhibiting the transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IFNγ and granzyme B as well as T H cell subset master transcription factors such as TBET and GATA-binding protein 3 (GATA3) 180-182 . TGFβ can also suppress the cytotoxic activity of CD8 + T cells and prevent their ability to traffic to tumours 183,184 . Through these actions, TGFβ suppresses the effector function of T eff cell subsets and their ability to respond to tumours.

## Direct cytolysis

Treg cells can use mechanisms involving direct contact with T eff cells to mediate suppression. This includes forming membrane gap junctions through which they transfer the cytolytic molecules perforin, granzyme A and granzyme B, leading to suppression through the killing of Teff cells 185,186 . T reg cells also transfer cAMP through these gap junctions to directly suppress T eff cells 187 .

## Targeting suppressive mechanisms

Treg cell population heterogeneity shows that not all T reg cells in the tumour use all suppressive mechanisms equally 174,188 . As we now understand more about the most suppressive subsets in multiple tumour types, targeting the mechanisms they preferentially use may provide more specificity. A limitation of this approach, however, would be how to target these therapies to tumour-specific T reg cells and avoid those in the periphery that might lead to a loss of immune tolerance. Thus far, no suppressive mechanisms have been identified that are solely used by tumour-specific Treg cells, so targeting them may result in unacceptable adverse side effects on immune homeostasis. However, the identification of novel immunosuppressive mechanisms used by Treg cells, particularly if these are selectively increased in the TME, could facilitate the discovery of new subpopulations with defining markers that can themselves be targeted. Given that genetic deletion or antibody-mediated blockade of one or two T reg cell-suppressive mechanisms often do not recapitulate the dramatic antitumour response that is unleashed when T reg cells are entirely depleted implies that additional suppressive mechanisms exist, which are yet to be discovered 11 .

## Recruitment and retention

Treg cells are recruited and then retained in the tumour may present viable therapeutic avenues. Mechanisms solely or primarily used by Treg cells as opposed to T eff cells would be attractive targets for blockade as they would alter the ratio of T cells from favouring suppression to favouring tumour elimination. Here we discuss expression of the chemokine receptors CCR4 and CCR8 by tumour-infiltrating T reg cells, both of which have been assessed for their clinical relevance with therapeutics in development aiming to specifically target tumour-infiltrating Treg cells for depletion. We then address additional chemokine-receptor pairings associated with T reg cell recruitment into tumours, which could provide further targeted therapeutic options.

The TME is an attractive location for the recruitment of T reg cells. Chemokines expressed both by tumour cells themselves and by macrophages in the tumour milieu (tumour-associated macrophages (TAMs)) as well as cancer-associated fibroblasts are responsible for recruiting T reg cells into the tumour based on their expression of chemokine receptors. For example, tumour-infiltrating T reg cells expressing high levels of CCR4 were first identified in patients with ovarian cancer and are specifically recruited into the tumour via their interaction with CC-chemokine ligand 22 (CCL22), expressed by both ovarian cancer cells and TAMs, highlighting a potential T reg cell recruitment mechanism for therapeutic targeting in tumours 28 . High levels of the CCR4 ligands CCL22 and CCL17 are produced by tumour cells, TAMs and DCs in multiple cancer types, including breast, lung and ovarian 189-193 . Indeed, the expression of CCL22 and CCL17 in human tumours correlates with increased Treg cell infiltration 194 . Furthermore, resistance to immunotherapy in various mouse models has also been associated with high levels of CCL22 and CCL17 production and recruitment of CCR4 + T reg cells into the tumour 194 . The relatively high CCR4 expression on a particularly suppressive subset of intratumoural Treg cells in multiple cancer types has led to its identification as a tumour-infiltrating T reg cell-specific marker and thus a possible therapeutic target either for depletion or antagonist therapy. In support of this, it has been shown that a CCR4 antibody administered to patients with melanoma can selectively deplete highly suppressive CCR4 + eT reg cells in the tumour and thus boost antitumour immunity 195 . Relatedly, a preclinical study recently showed that CCR4 expression by Treg cells is crucial for their recruitment into the perilymphatic region of tumour stroma through interactions with DCs enriched in immunoregulatory molecules (mregDCs), such that conditional deletion of Ccr4 on T reg cells significantly reduced tumour growth 196 . CCR4-dependent recruitment of T reg cells results in the formation of a T reg -mregDC cell niche in the perilymphatics, where T reg cells acquire an activated and suppressive phenotype that inhibits mregDC migration to draining lymph nodes and results in decreased presentation of tumour antigens, decreasing antitumour immunity.

Similarly, the chemokine receptor CCR8 is a highly specific marker of tumour-infiltrating T reg cells across cancer types 197-200 . Expression of its ligand CCL1 by cancer cells, TAMs and cancer-associated fibroblasts recruits CCR8 + T reg cells into the tumour, and this interaction is involved in the conversion of CD4 + T cells into T reg cells 201-206 . Approximately 50% of tumour-infiltrating T reg cells express CCR8, and it also marks a highly suppressive population of T reg cells with a more activated phenotype, indicating it as a therapeutic target 57,207 . Indeed, using CCR8 as a target for therapeutic depletion results in tumour regression with specific loss of T reg cells in tumours and minimal adverse effects 197,198 . Yet, Treg cell-specific deletion of CCR8 does not substantially alter T reg cell infiltration into the tumour or suppressive function, suggesting that therapeutic targeting intended to halt migration of this population may not be efficacious 208 . Thus, CCR8 expression may be downstream of acquisition of suppressive functions. In addition, although this data suggest that CCR8 does not have a role in T reg cell recruitment to the tumour, it may still have important functions for retention, as upregulation could occur after T reg cells have already entered the tumour to prevent them from trafficking out. Studies using inducible deletion of CCR8 + cells in tumour-bearing mice could address these questions. Furthermore, although this shows why antibody therapies aiming to block CCR8 may not be efficacious, as CCR8 marks highly suppressive Treg cells and is specific to the tumour, it supports the development and use of depleting antibodies.

Several other chemokine-receptor pairings are currently of interest. Global gene expression profiling of tumour-infiltrating myeloid-derived suppressor cells from mouse and human samples identified increased expression of CCR5 ligands including CCL3, CCL4 and CCL5, with expression of CCR5 preferentially detected on tumour-associated Treg cells and shown to mediate recruitment of these cells in vitro and in vivo 209 . Indeed, blockade of either CCR5 or its ligand CCL5 limits entry of T reg cells into pancreatic and squamous cell carcinomas, indicating that the CCR5-CCL5 interaction is important for T reg cell recruitment into tumours 210,211 . In ovarian cancer, hypoxia within tumours leads to upregulation of CCL28, the ligand for CCR10, recruiting CCR10 + T reg cells into the tumour 212 .

Expression of CXC-chemokine ligand 12 (CXCL12) in the TME is important for promoting angiogenesis, survival of tumour cells and recruitment of cells including T reg cells to promote an immunosuppressive environment 213 . Secretion of this chemokine has been identified in multiple tumour types, including cervical, mesothelioma, ovarian and renal cell carcinoma 214-218 . T reg cells and TAMs expressing high levels of its receptor CXCR4 are recruited to tumours where they generate a suppressive microenvironment 219 , and so interrupting this recruitment could have therapeutic utility. For example, ovarian tumour-bearing mice treated with the CXCR4 antagonist AMD3100 have fewer intratumoural Treg cells, and this treatment results in their conversion into TH cells 216 , indicating that therapeutic disruption of chemokine interactions may not only reduce T reg cell migration into the tumour but also alter their function.

The expression of several other chemokines in the TME or chemokine receptors on tumour-infiltrating T reg cells is associated with high levels of T reg cell recruitment in different cancer types. For example, in a mouse colorectal cancer model, increased CCL20 expression by TAMs is associated with recruitment of T reg cells expressing CCR6 to promote tumour growth 220 . In human ovarian cancer, recruitment of T reg cells expressing CXCR3 is associated with restriction of type I immunity 221 . Several other tumour types have increased numbers of CXCR3 + T reg cells, and expression of this molecule can promote colocalization with CXCL9-producing DCs 222 . DC1-Treg cell interactions can restrain DC-CD8 + T cell interactions and thus reduce antigen cross-presentation and CD8 + T cell priming, reducing antitumour immunity. Further studies are needed to identify whether these represent viable targets for therapeutic intervention. In addition, human Treg cells are recruited by tumour-specific antigens expressed in the context of TCR-MHC-II interactions, in which T reg cell clones were identified due to their ability to recognize an antigen derived from a mutated peptide found only in the tumour 223 . This was linked to preferential recruitment and activation of tumour-specific T reg cells within the tumour. Once they have migrated from the periphery, T reg cells can be retained in the tumour if they downregulate lymphoid homing receptors such as CCR7 and CD62L (also known as L-selectin) 16 .

The key to developing therapies in this area is a greater understanding of how T reg cell trafficking to the tumour differs from that of CD8 + T cells. For example, a recent study used an antigen receptor signalling reporter mouse to fate-map lymphocytes responding to tumour antigens. They found that while intratumoural antigen signalling traps CD8 + T cells in the tumour, leading to clonal expansion and exhaustion, T reg cell clonal populations responding to tumour antigens can recirculate out of the tumour and do not develop an exhausted phenotype 224 . Therefore, the development of therapies that prevent Treg cell entry into tumours while enabling CD8 + T cell cycling could promote tumour clearance by removing T reg cell-mediated suppression and possibly preventing CD8 + T cell exhaustion. This could take the form of bispecific antibody strategies, which guide the targeting of chemokine receptor blockade specifically to T reg cell receptors highly associated with migration to the TME and would also require detailed investigation of the differences between migration mechanisms used by CD8 + T cells versus T reg cells, possibly using spatial sequencing approaches.

Mechanisms that govern retention of T reg cells in the tumour or cause them to circulate between the tumour and periphery remain unknown; however, a recent in vivo photolabelling study has shown that there is considerable heterogeneity within the T reg cell population in terms of which cells remain in the tumour compared with those that migrate in and out 225 . Several T reg cell clusters were identified, and those retained in the tumour express higher levels of molecules related to immunosuppressive function (for example, CD25, LAG3, CTLA4 and IL-10). However, expression of neuropilin 1 (NRP1) was inversely correlated with immunosuppressive function in intratumoural T reg cells. Yet, the identified clusters only represented half of all T reg cells migrating into the tumour, meaning that there are still considerable unknowns surrounding the mechanisms that drive Treg cell migration to and retention in the tumour, which could provide therapeutic avenues to promote turnover of T reg cells in tumours.

Therapies that target recruitment should focus on mechanisms used exclusively by T reg cells, which could conceivably be used to alter the balance between suppressive and pro-inflammatory cell populations to promote tumour clearance. However, as there may be some redundancy that allows T reg cells to be recruited by a secondary mechanism, combination therapies may be required. In addition, it may be possible to use localization mechanisms identified from T reg cells in novel CAR-T cell approaches to enhance recruitment to the tumour, for example, by inducing overexpression of certain chemokine receptors. New approaches such as spatial transcriptomics could be used to identify cell-cell interactions in the tumour that promote T reg cell retention and could be compared with interactions of T eff cells to identify viable therapeutic targets. Given the preferential exclusion of CD8 + T cells from the TME compared with Treg cells, it is possible that there are differences between their recruitment and retention mechanisms that have yet to be fully elucidated. However, these differences could be downstream of chemokine receptor signalling and whether they can be targeted therapeutically remains an outstanding question.

## Maintenance and metabolic adaptation

The deregulation of metabolism is a hallmark of cancer - the nutrient-deficient, hypoxic and acidic TME can promote tumour progression, partially through its ability to deregulate the immune response 226 . T reg cells have a metabolic profile distinct from T eff cells that allows them to retain functionality in the tumour and promote a suppressive microenvironment 227 . Indeed, conditions in the TME and the presence of certain metabolites can hinder the function of T eff cells while maintaining tumour-infiltrating T reg cell functionality 228-231 . Tumour-infiltrating T reg cells can reprogram their metabolism to adapt to the TME and maintain suppressive function, which could make it feasible to specifically target tumour-infiltrating T reg cell metabolism 230-233 . In this section, we discuss how features of the TME reprogram T reg cell metabolism and alter cell phenotype. We also highlight how T reg cells adapt to the TME better than T eff cells and how these mechanisms can be used to improve immunotherapeutic drug efficacy (Fig. 3).

## Glucose metabolism

In both normal and tumour tissues, T reg cells with high glucose uptake correlate with poorer Treg cell functionality 227 . In normal tissues, signalling via MYC and mTORC1 is important for the formation of eTreg cells and for promoting their suppressive function 234-238 . Although PI3K-mTORC2-mediated glycolysis is required for T reg cell migration into the tumour, this is not the case for mediation of the suppressive functions of cells once within the TME 239,240 . Tumour-infiltrating T reg cells can shift from glycolysis to oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), driven by FOXP3-mediated suppression of the MYC and PI3K-AKT-mTORC1 signalling pathways 241,242 . FOXP3 also can reprogram T reg cells to increase their capacity to use fatty acids in OXPHOS by upregulating expression of components of the electron transport chain 243 . By contrast, T eff cells are functionally constrained due to their reliance on glycolysis for effector responses 244 . As glucose is relatively scarce in the TME, the ability of Treg cells to metabolize other substrates (for example, lactate and fatty acids) allows them to metabolically outcompete T eff cells and provides a functional advantage that helps to drive tumour progression 245 .

Many tumour cells and effector immune cells are highly glycolytic, which leads to the accumulation of the byproducts lactate and lactic acid 246 . High concentrations of lactate can impair T eff cell function in the TME through lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)-mediated NAD depletion, impairing the NAD-dependent metabolic reactions required for T eff cell proliferation 247 . More recently, however, lactate alone has been shown to promote CD8 + T cell-mediated antitumour immunity, whereas lactic acid is responsible for immunosuppression through inducing CD8 + T cell death as a result of low pH 248 . By contrast, T reg cells are resistant to lactate-mediated suppression as FOXP3 reduces glycolysis, induces OXPHOS and increases the NAD:NADH ratio 242 . The presence of glucose in the TME has a negative effect on T reg cell function and is involved in the promotion of T reg cell function and is involved in the promotion of T reg cell fragility 103,227 . In vitro assays using the fluorescent glucose tracer-2-NBDG assessed differences between Treg cells that took up high versus low levels of glucose. Enrichment of genes associated with lactic acid metabolism was observed in 2-NBDG low T reg cells, including upregulated expression of the lactate receptor monocarboxylate transporter 1 (MCT1) and LDH, which enables them to convert to lactic acid metabolism and thus adapt to TME conditions 227 . Accordingly, both constitutive and inducible deletion of the MCT1 gene Slc16a1 in mice is required for suppression by T reg cells in the tumour but is dispensable in peripheral T reg cells. Interestingly, T reg cells cannot take up a high amount of both lactic acid and glucose at the same time, which might indicate a reciprocal negative regulatory role for lactic acid and glucose in controlling the uptake of the other. Thus, lactic acid may promote Treg cell suppressive functions by preventing glucose uptake; however, the biological mechanism for this has yet to be identified. Furthermore, extracellular lactic acid in tumours is capable of inducing TGFβ-mediated FOXP3 expression in CD4 + T conv cells, generating pTreg cells with suppressive functions 249 . ## Fatty acid metabolism

The TME also contains an abundance of fatty acids 253 . The ability of Treg cells to metabolize fatty acids using glycolysis and OXPHOS gives them a metabolic advantage over T eff cells in the TME 240 . For example, in gastric cancer, the increased production of free fatty acids increased Treg cell accumulation in the TME due to more effective consumption of this substrate than T eff cells 254 . In addition, fatty acid metabolism by Treg cells in tumours results in a proliferative and survival advantage over T eff cells due to enhanced lipid uptake and fatty acid synthesis 255 . A byproduct of lipid metabolism is lipid peroxidation, which results in ferroptosis and could inhibit T reg cell survival; however, expression of the selenoenzyme glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) maintains redox homeostasis in tumour-infiltrating T reg cells 256 . By contrast, the accumulation of free fatty acids in the TME drives CD8 + T cell dysfunction through impairing mitochondrial function and inducing transcriptional reprogramming of pathways involved in lipid metabolism 257-259 .

Tumour-infiltrating T reg cell reliance on fatty acid metabolism is emphasized by the loss of immunosuppressive capabilities and metabolic fitness when fatty acid uptake, oxidation or synthesis is disrupted 131,240 . The fatty acid transporter CD36 is upregulated in multiple cancer types and associated with poor clinical outcomes 260 . Genetic ablation of Cd36 specifically in T reg cells reduced tumour growth due to fewer T reg cells in the tumour and enhanced responses in T eff cells in a manner that did not result in widespread immune dysregulation 261 . Furthermore, the lipid chaperone fatty acid-binding protein 5 (FABP5) is responsible for mitochondrial integrity in T reg cells, such that inhibition of its function causes mitochondrial changes that enhance suppression 262 . In the gut, short-chain fatty acids produced by bacteria promote pTreg cell generation and function via G protein-coupled receptor 43 (GPCR43) 263 . Bile acid metabolites, which are converted by gut commensal bacteria, bind to nuclear receptors and/or GPCRs and promote colonic RORγ +  pTreg cell generation through various mechanisms 264-267 , which may be important for cancers at mucosal sites. Among them, isoallolithocholic acid, a lithocholic acid derivative, induces mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) and FOXP3 expression 265,267 through nuclear receptor subfamily 4 group A member 1 (NR4A1) 267 . By contrast, 3β-hydroxydeoxycholic acid, indirectly promotes FOXP3 expression in pT reg cells by modulating DCs 266 . Therefore, the link between fatty acid metabolism and T reg cell function indicates that further exploration of this pathway could identify new immunotherapeutic targets.

## Other metabolites

Several other metabolites in the TME regulate tumour-infiltrating Treg cells 231 .  Adenosine is a suppressive molecule generated by Treg cells through the action of CD39 and CD73, yet it can also stimulate T reg cells directly via interaction with the A 2A adenosine receptor on the T reg cell surface to promote proliferation and suppressive functions. This is in contrast to the effect of adenosine on CD8 + T cells, where A 2A receptor binding causes inhibition of effector functions 270 . In addition, serine and palmitic acid levels are elevated in the TME. These metabolites are substrates for the sphingolipid synthesis pathway, and increased levels are associated with increased T reg cell differentiation and accumulation in tumours 271 . In homeostatic conditions, amino acids and their metabolites influence the activation, suppressive functions and metabolic fitness of T reg cells. Examples include the immunosuppressive tryptophan catabolite kynurenine, which acts on Treg cells through the ligand-activated transcription factor aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) to promote T reg cell generation and activation, and glutamine, the presence of which results in the conversion of T H 1 cells into T reg cells 272-274 . Conversely, serine interferes with T reg cell-mediated suppression and FOXP3 expression, and limiting its availability preserves T reg cell function 275 . TCR signalling induces upregulation of amino acid transporters in activated eT reg cells, and results in mTORC1 activation via Ras-related GTP-binding protein A (RAGA) and RAGB and RHEB1 and RHEB2 GTPase activity, the deficiency of which results in reduced functionality, cellular fitness and recruitment 276,277 . For example, deletion of the lactate transporter MCT1 in T reg cells can attenuate their suppressive functions and enhance the efficacy of anti-PD1 therapy 227,250 . Accordingly, pharmacological inhibition of MCT1 using the small-molecule inhibitor AZD3965 is in phase I clinical trials for various tumour types 282 . A caveat here is that any metabolic targets identified through cell-specific deletion and then targeted by small-molecule inhibitors may result in unacceptable effects on other cells that also express those targets in non-tumour tissue. Given that metabolic pathways are often highly conserved, it may be challenging to find a way to focus their action on T reg cells in tumours. Treatment with high doses of etomoxir, a long-chain fatty acid oxidation inhibitor, prevented induced Treg cell differentiation and proliferation using a mechanism that reduced oxygen consumption rate by altering mitochondrial function 283 . Originally attributed to the inhibition of carnitine O -palmitoyltransferase 1A (CPT1A), an enzyme that oxidizes fatty acids, this independent mechanism was discovered as an off-target effect. Therefore, care should be taken when identifying new metabolic targets to ensure that reagent dosage is optimized to avoid off-target effects and that in vitro findings can be replicated in vivo. Finally, many metabolic processes occur in the cytosol, so consideration needs to be given to how therapies will gain access to their targets, which in this case favours a small-molecule inhibitor approach.

## Concluding remarks

Treg cells are key regulators of immune homeostasis and barriers to autoimmunity, although their contribution to antitumour immunity cannot be overstated. Immunotherapeutically targeting T reg cells will ultimately rely on a greater understanding of their biology within tumours, including how to effectively target only tumour-infiltrating T reg cells, sparing Teff cells or T reg cells present in other tissues. Consideration must also be given to the therapeutic mechanism that invokes the best efficacy. Is it depleting tumour-infiltrating T reg cells, preventing their recruitment, destabilizing their function or a combination of these approaches? Examples of targets relating to all of these have been identified and some have shown therapeutic promise, although there is potential for more opportunities coming through the discovery pipeline (Fig. 4).

Combining single-cell transcriptomic analysis with new subcellular resolution spatial sequencing approaches could aid the discovery of novel T reg cell subpopulations within the TME and identify markers for therapeutic targeting. Technological advancements in CRISPR-screening approaches, such as combinations with single-cell RNA sequencing (for example, Perturb sequencing 284,285 ), would facilitate the rapid testing of multiple candidate genes for T reg cell functional modulation to identify the optimal therapeutic approach.

Successful targeting of unique T reg cell subpopulations using novel marker combinations may require the development of novel bispecific antibody therapies. This may facilitate the selective targeting of cells expressing two markers, sparing those only expressing one. This approach is attractive considering the difficulty of identifying markers that exclusively identify tumour-associated T reg cells and no other cell population. For example, a recent mouse study investigated a bispecific antibody targeting OX40 and 4-1BB, already preclinically shown to enhance antitumour activity of CD8 + T cells, on T reg cells.

Rather than enhancing T reg cell suppressive activity, treatment resulted in T reg cell fragility and lineage instability, resulting in IFNγ production and reduced FOXP3 expression, respectively 95 . This study provides support for an immunotherapeutic approach that has a differential effect on T reg cells versus T eff cells, despite both expressing the same surface molecules.

To date, most therapeutic strategies targeting tumour-infiltrating Treg cells have ultimately been unsuccessful in the clinic, either because they cannot sufficiently reduce immunosuppression in the tumour or because they result in autoimmune or other side effects. Often, achieving the intended result does not sufficiently increase antitumour immunity. Indeed, a recent study showed that one limitation to the promotion of antitumour immunity upon depletion of T reg cells in the tumour is associated with T conv cells acquiring suppressive function through a compensatory mechanism involving the upregulation of IL-10 expression 286 . Therefore, it is important to consider both the optimal therapeutic outcome and the possible therapeutic   combinations to target both primary and secondary suppressive mechanisms.

Future therapeutic targets may include those that are less conventional. Despite the importance of transcription factors in determining tumour-infiltrating T reg cell fate and function, they have not been previously considered as viable druggable targets due to challenges of access and specificity. CRISPR-screening approaches identified the zinc-finger transcription factor TRPS1 as a master regulator of tumour-infiltrating Treg cells and that gemcitabine, a pre-existing chemotherapeutic drug, inhibited TRPS1 transcriptional activity 287 . This provides proof of concept that therapeutic targeting of transcription factors may be a viable option, especially if more specific small-molecule inhibitors can be developed. Targeting epigenetic regulators may result in specific disruption of tumour-infiltrating T reg cell fitness. For example, a small-molecule inhibitor of JMJD1C, a histone demethylase specifically expressed in intratumoural T reg cells, resulted in heightened AKT signalling and T reg cell fragility 288 . Another therapeutic strategy could be to indirectly target tumour-infiltrating T reg cells. Platelet factor 4 is a chemokine expressed by TAMs that promotes Treg cell polarization into a more suppressive phenotype. Antibody-mediated neutralization restricted this process and resulted in enhanced antitumour immunity in mice 289 . Thus, alternative therapeutic approaches that selectively target tumour-infiltrating T reg cells should be considered to enhance the efficacy of existing strategies.

Given the resistance that some patients exhibit to current immunotherapies, it is essential to identify new therapeutic targets in immune cells beyond CD8 + T cells within the TME. T reg cells present a promising alternative, and recent studies have expanded our knowledge of their behaviour in the TME. Although there is still considerable progress to be made in translating these findings into effective treatments, sustained research focused on tumour-infiltrating T reg cells from both a fundamental and a translational perspective is crucial.
