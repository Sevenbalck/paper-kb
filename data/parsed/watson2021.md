Regulatory T (T reg ) cells, although vital for immune homeostasis, also represent a major barrier to anti-cancer immunity, as the tumour microenvironment (TME) promotes the recruitment, differentiation and activity of these cells 1,2 . Tumour cells show deregulated metabolism, leading to a metabolite-depleted, hypoxic and acidic TME 3 , which places infiltrating effector T cells in competition with the tumour for metabolites and impairs their function 4-6 . At the same time, T reg cells maintain a strong suppression of effector T cells within the TME 7,8 . As previous studies suggested that Treg cells possess a distinct metabolic profile from effector T cells 9-11 , we hypothesized that the altered metabolic landscape of the TME and increased activity of intratumoral T reg cells are linked. Here we show that T reg cells display broad heterogeneity in their metabolism of glucose within normal and transformed tissues, and can engage an alternative metabolic pathway to maintain suppressive function and proliferation. Glucose uptake correlates with poorer suppressive function and long-term instability, and high-glucose conditions impair the function and stability of Treg cells in vitro. T reg cells instead upregulate pathways involved in the metabolism of the glycolytic by-product lactic acid. T reg cells withstand high-lactate conditions, and treatment with lactate prevents the destabilizing effects of high-glucose conditions, generating intermediates necessary for proliferation. Deletion of MCT1-a lactate transporter-in T reg cells reveals that lactate uptake is dispensable for the function of peripheral T reg cells but required intratumorally, resulting in slowed tumour growth and an increased response to immunotherapy. Thus, T reg cells are metabolically flexible: they can use 'alternative' metabolites in the TME to maintain their suppressive identity. Further, our results suggest that tumours avoid destruction by not only depriving effector T cells of nutrients, but also metabolically supporting regulatory populations.

As it was previously shown that heightened metabolism in tumour cells correlates with decreased function of effector T cells 5 , we sought to determine the relationship between heightened tumour metabolism and T reg cell function. We measured the suppressor activity of Treg cells isolated from Foxp3 reporter mice 12,13 bearing tumours of increasing glycolytic capacity, revealing that glycolysis by tumour cells correlated with the suppressive function of intratumoral T reg cells (Fig. 1a). As expected, T reg cells were enriched within tumours (Fig. 1b, Extended Data Fig. 1a, b) and were not metabolically suppressed but actively proliferating (Fig. 1c). Given this relationship between tumour glycolysis and the function of T reg cells, we determined the glycolytic capacity of T reg cells directly ex vivo and immediately after activation-a reprogramming event that occurs downstream of T cell antigen receptors (TCRs) in T cells 14,15 . TCR triggering induced less glycolysis in T reg cells compared with naive or antigen-experienced conventional T cells (Fig. 1d). This resistance to glycolysis persisted under strong activating stimuli (anti-CD3 or anti-CD28 antibodies plus interleukin (IL)-2 for 48 h), and tumour-derived T reg cells displayed even lower glycolytic activity (Extended Data Fig. 1c). Treatment with oligomycin showed that T reg cells are resistant to engaging glycolysis even when oxidative metabolism is inhibited, suggesting that glucose transport may be limiting in T reg cells (Extended Data Fig. 1c). ## Glucose avidity predicts T reg function

We next purified Treg subsets of low and high glucose avidity and measured their function. Regardless of tissue, 2NBDG-avid T reg cells showed significantly reduced suppressive capacity compared with 2NBDG lo T reg cells (Fig. 2a, Extended Data Fig. 2a); this finding was not attributable to viability, as 2NBDG hi T reg cells were rather more viable after the assay (Extended Data Fig. 2b, c), and retained their initial proclivities for glucose (Extended Data Fig. 2b, d). Thus, within a tissue, avidity for glucose predicts poorly suppressive T reg cells. Sequencing of 2NBDG lo and 2NBDG hi T reg cells that infiltrate lymph nodes and B16 melanomas revealed reduced expression of Foxp3 , Ikzf2 (Helios), Il2ra (CD25), Nrp1 and other T reg signature genes in 2NBDG hi cells; this was further confirmed by flow cytometry (Fig. 2b, Extended Data Fig. 2e).

These data suggest while glucose-avid T reg cells are still Foxp3 + , they harbour a 'weaker' T reg signature. As 2NBDG hi T reg cells had some suppressor function, we measured cytokine production after a brief restimulation. 2NBDG hi T reg cells produced significantly more IL-10 than did 2NBDG lo cells, suggesting distinct suppressive mechanisms between 2NBDG T reg states-especially intriguing given their differences in gene expression (Fig. 2b, c). These differences may be driven by glycolysis, which is tied to cytokine translation in conventional T cells 18 .

To assess the long-term stability and function of different subsets of 2NBDG Treg cells under inflammatory conditions, we transferred Thy1.2 + Foxp3 + T reg cells of high or low glucose avidity into Rag1 -deficient mice, along with Thy1.1 + Foxp3 T conv cells to induce colitis. After 7 weeks, mice receiving glucose-avid T reg cells began to lose weight owing to increased colitis compared with those receiving 2NBDG lo T reg cells, and transferred 2NBDG hi T reg cells lost Foxp3 expression (Fig. 2d, e, Extended Data Fig. 2f). We also investigated whether increased glucose uptake could be observed in T reg cells from an unmanipulated autoimmune setting. T reg cells isolated from pancreatic islets (known to be dysfunctional 19 ) and draining lymph node of prediabetic NOD. Foxp3 GFP mice took up significantly more glucose than those from non-draining lymph node (ndLN) (Fig. 2f). Thus, glucose avidity in T reg cells correlates with poor suppressive capacity, ultimately leading to instability.

We next asked whether exposure to a high-glucose environment would drive a less-suppressive phenotype, regardless of glucose uptake status. Notably, conditioning T reg cells in high glucose dampened their suppressive function when later assayed in isoglycaemic conditions (Fig. 2g, Extended Data Fig. 2g). We further investigated the transcriptome of 2NBDG lo T reg cells to identify metabolic pathways that may support their function. 2NBDG lo T reg cells did not possess a transcriptome that suggested preferential lipid metabolism; rather, lipid-metabolism genes were enriched in the 2NBDG hi subset (Extended Data Fig. 2h). Both subsets expressed upstream enzymes in the glycolytic pathway, suggesting that proclivity for glucose was not regulated at the level of transporter or enzyme expression (Fig. 2h). The 2NBDG lo subpopulation, however, showed enriched expression of enzymes involved in the terminal steps of glycolysis, specifically lactate dehydrogenase (encoded by Ldha ) and the monocarboxylate transporter MCT1 (encoded by Slc16a1 ) (Fig. 2h), confirmed by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) (Extended Data Fig. 2i). Although lactate is the end-product of glycolysis, it represents a major fuel source for many cells 20 . Lactate uptake is mediated through MCT1, where it is converted by LDH to pyruvate 21 . We found that T reg cells (but not T conv cells) conditioned in low-glucose or glucose-deficient media increased expression of Ldha and Slc16a1 (Extended Data Fig. 2j). Thus, T reg cells upregulate genes involved in lactate metabolism.

Lactate can stimulate cells via the G-protein-coupled receptor GPR81 (ref. 25 ) and enter as a metabolite via MCT1, so-by measuring changes in intracellular pH-we investigated whether T reg cells could consume lactic acid 26 . Thus T reg cells not only are resistant to lactic acid but can also take up this metabolite, and those that do display a signature of activated T reg cells and low glucose uptake.

To identify how Treg cells were using lactic acid, we performed high-resolution mass spectrometry of activated T reg and T conv cells pulsed with [U 13 C]l-lactate (at pH 6.9) (Fig. 3f). We used lymph-node-derived Treg cells (which do readily take up lactate) (Fig. 3b) to facilitate the cellular input needed for mass-spectrometry analysis, confirming that T reg cells took up significantly more lactate than T conv cells (Fig. 3g). T reg cells converted 13 C-lactate into pyruvate and subsequently into citrate and malate, indicating mitochondrial import and entry to the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle (Fig. 3g). Further analysis revealed that T reg cells also incorporated lactate-derived carbon into phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) (Fig. 3h), formed by phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK) when malate leaves the mitochondria and is converted to oxaloacetate. PEP can contribute to upstream glycolytic intermediates essential for proliferation, suggesting that lactate may serve as a gluconeogenic fuel source, decreasing a T reg cell's need for glucose. As high glucose can inhibit the suppressor function of T reg cells (Fig. 2g), we investigated whether lactic acid could mitigate the deleterious effects of glucose. Indeed, conditioning T reg cells in high glucose plus tumour-equivalent concentrations of lactic acid maintained suppressor function (Fig. 3i).

## Lactic acid supports intratumoral T reg cells

To determine the significance of lactic acid use by T reg cells in vivo, we generated a constitutive, T reg -specific deletion of MCT1 ( Slc16a1 f/f Foxp3 cre ). qPCR confirmed the deletion of Slc16a1 , a lack of compensation by upregulation of Slc16a7 /MCT2 or Slc16a3 /MCT4, and no deletion in CD4 + Foxp3 cells (Extended Data Fig. 4a, b). As expected, deletion of Slc16a1 resulted in loss of lactic acid uptake (Fig. 4a). These mice did not show any overt signs of autoimmunity or lymphoproliferation (Extended Data Fig. 4c, d). Rather, T reg cells from the lymph nodes of Slc16a1 f/f Foxp3 YFPcre mice displayed similar suppressive capacity, proliferation and expression of T reg signature genes to wild-type mice when assayed ex vivo (Extended Data Fig. 4e-h). MCT1 deficiency in T reg cells revealed no significant differences in the percentage of Foxp3 + cells infiltrating various tissues, although several compartments showed increases in glucose uptake at steady state (Extended Data Fig. 4i, j). Inoculating Slc16a1 f/f Foxp3 cre mice with B16 melanoma resulted in slowed tumour growth and prolonged survival (Fig. 4b). To remove any unforeseen effects of deleting MCT1 on the development of T reg cells, we interrupted MCT1 expression just before injecting B16 melanoma cells, by using a tamoxifen-inducible, T reg -specific model ( Slc16a1 f/f Foxp3 cre.ERT2 ), finding immunological and survival outcomes similar to those in the non-inducible model, including the development of a 'fragile' 29 T reg phenotype in which Foxp3 + cells begin expressing IFNγ (Extended Data Fig. 5). We thus reasoned that loss of lactate uptake in T reg cells produced an environment conducive to immunotherapy. Triggering deletion of MCT1 in T reg cells synergized with anti-PD-1 therapy, resulting in complete tumour regressions in 37.5% of B16-bearing mice (which are typically insensitive to anti-PD-1 therapy) (Fig. 4l). Deletion of MCT1 from Tconv cells using Slc16a1 f/f Cd4 cre mice did not affect their capacity to produce IL-2 and IFNγ, suggesting that MCT1 is not generally required for the function of T cells (Extended Data Fig. 4q). Thus, the expression of MCT1 and consequent uptake of lactic acid are dispensable for most tissue-derived T reg cells, but required to maintain their high suppressor activity in the TME.

Our study highlights the antagonistic effects of glucose on the function and identity of T reg cells. Although previous data support this 9,10,23,30 , some studies using transcriptomic and proteomic analysis have suggested that T reg cells are highly glycolytic 31-33 . We show that the consumption of glucose by T reg cells is certainly heterogeneous both between tissues (Fig. 1g) and within the T reg population (Fig. 2a); however, glucose uptake characterizes poorly suppressive T reg cells, or at least those that are suppressing through mechanisms not potent in vitro (Fig. 2a, c, Extended Data Fig. 2a). Notably, T reg cells consuming low amounts of glucose still express genes of the glycolytic pathway (Fig. 2h). As most reactions in glycolysis are reversible, T reg cells probably build higher-order intermediates from carbon sources such as lactate (Fig. 3).

Lactate is often thought of as a waste product of glycolytic metabolism, but it represents an important metabolite for cellular function and a likely fuel source for tissues 20 . Our data suggest that T reg cells do not require lactic acid for survival, but are endowed with metabolic flexibility to use this carbon source, both as fuel and as a means to protect their high suppressive capacity from the negative effects of glucose (Fig. 3i). Consequently, T reg cells cannot uptake large amounts of both lactic acid and glucose (Figs. 1g, 2f, 3c, d, Extended Data Fig. 3d). Thus, we propose that a glucose-lactate axis exists to fine-tune the functioning of T reg cells depending on the nutrient milieu (Figs. 2g, 3i). Lactate not only fuels the TCA cycle but is also exported from the mitochondria, contributing to higher glycolytic pathways via PEP. This gluconeogenic component of lactate metabolism supports the proliferation of T reg cells, essential in a cell type that does not readily take up glucose. However, the contribution of PEPCK-independent lactate metabolism appears also to be at the level of suppression of Treg cells (Fig. 4f), suggesting that lactate has a multipronged role in the biology of T reg cells.

It is unlikely that regulatory T cells evolved to thrive in tumours; rather, cancers exploit this predilection for alternative substrates to maintain an immunosuppressive environment. Lactate is not only enriched in the tumour but also elevated in immunologically distinct tissue environments such as muscles, adipose tissue and the nervous system 34,35 . We also suspect that lactate is one of a host of alternative metabolites that T reg cells can use. MCT1 itself can also transport acetate, succinate, propionate and butyrate, which have been shown to have roles in T reg cells 21,36,37 . Indeed, our findings using transfer colitis suggest that MCT1 deficiency may affect the function of T reg cells in the gut. Notably, however, we did not observe overt gut inflammation at the steady state in Slc16a1 f/f Foxp3 cre mice, suggesting that both inflammation and the availability of MCT1 substrates have roles in maintaining Treg cells in that environment.

Our study suggests that, as T reg cells must be active when immune activity is at its lowest, they exist metabolically 'out-of-sync' with their conventional counterparts. This requires the use of fuels prevalent in tissue environments to maintain suppressor function when their conventional T cell targets may be competing for nutrients. As lactate can promote the suppressive functions of other tolerogenic cell types, such as tumour-associated macrophages 38 , cell types that share lactate metabolism may share suppressor activity. Although this is exploited in the tumour microenvironment, causing suppressive cells to thrive, it is actionable: T reg -specific deletion of the lactate transporter results not only in decreased tumour growth but also in synergy with checkpoint blockade immunotherapy. Inhibition of MCT1, to directly target lactate metabolism, or inhibition of tumour acidity may break this metabolic symbiosis and lower the T reg barrier to cancer immunity.

## Article Methods

## In vitro tissue culture

B16-F10 cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). MC38 cells were obtained from D.A.A.V. (commercially available from Kerafast). Clone24 was originally produced by single-cell sorting from melanoma tumours arising from Pten f/f Braf LSL-V600E Tyr creER mice 5 . MEER cells were obtained from R. Ferris (originally from ref. 39 ). The B16 cell line was authenticated in 2018 through independent sequencing by the supplier. MC38 cells were authenticated in 2016 through independent sequencing by the supplier. Clone24 was authenticated in house via metabolic profiling and tumour growth compared with Melan-A cells (normal melanocytes) in 2019 (ref. 5 ). MEER cells, generated by overexpressing E6/E7 and Ras in primary mouse tonsil epithelial cells (MTECs), were authenticated by the supplier in 2013 via immunoblot to confirm E6/E7 and Ras overexpression. MC38 and MEER were confirmed mycoplasma free in 2016, B16 in 2018, and Clone24 in 2019. Both primary and immortalized cell lines were maintained in laboratory-made R10 medium (RPMI 1640, 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 2 mM l-glutamine, penicillin plus streptomycin, non-essential amino acids, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 5 mM HEPES and β-mercaptoethanol). Cultures were incubated in temperature-stable and partial-pressure-stable conditions at 37 °C and 5% CO 2 . For in vitro conditioning experiments, isolated T reg or T conv cells were activated in complete R10 medium with 0.1 μg ml -1 phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) and 1 μg ml -1 ionomycin (Sigma-Aldrich) with 1,000 U ml -1 IL-2 for roughly 20 h at 37 °C, then placed in the conditions indicated in Figs. 2g, 3i and Extended Data Figs. 2j, 3a-c, i, j with 1,000 U ml -1 IL-2. For experiments involving 3MP (Cayman Chemical) in vitro, a concentration of 250 μM was used.

## In vivo mouse studies

Sample sizes were not statistically predetermined but were chosen on the basis of previous T reg functional assays 8,29  and tumour immunology 4,5,8,14,29 . These sample sizes are sufficient because they allowed for the determination of statistical significance between groups and minimized the number of mice or replicates needed for each experiment. Mice were placed into experimental groups by nature of their genotype, and/or if receiving treatment were randomized within a genotype. For experiments not involving mice, cells were randomized into experimental groups. Blinding was not possible as most of the data acquisition and analysis was carried out by a single person; having two individuals perform every experiment was not feasible during the course of our study. C57BL/6JFoxp3 YFPicre , -Foxp3 GFP.cre.ERT2 , -Rag1 -/-and -Thy1 a (Thy1.1 congenic) mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory. Slc16a1 f/f mice were a gift from J. Rothstein and B. Morrison 40 , and were used to generate Slc16a1 f/f Foxp3 GFP.cre.ERT2 (termed Slc16a1 f/f Foxp3 cre-ERT2 ) and Slc16a1 f/f Foxp3 YFP.cre (termed Slc16a1 f/f Foxp3 cre ) mice. Foxp3 FlpO Ametrine mice were a gift from D.A.A.V. Spleens of OT-II Foxp3 RFP Thy1.1 + mice were a gift from G. Camirand. Mouse work was done in accordance with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Pittsburgh (protocol17071235). All mice were housed in specific-pathogen-free conditions at an ambient temperature of 20-26 °C and humidity of 30-70% with a 12 h:12 h light:dark cycle before use. A maximal tumour size of 15 mm in any direction was not exceeded in any experiment. Both male and female mice were used in studies, from five to ten weeks of age.

## Flow sorting and cytometric analysis

Single-cell suspensions from mouse tumours (day 14 post tumour inoculation), lymph nodes, liver, thymus or spleen were derived through mechanical separation and passage through a 70-μm filter (Fisherbrand). For detection of hypoxia, mice were injected intravenously with pimonidazole (80 mg kg -1 ; Hypoxyprobe) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) one hour before being killed. Tumour dissolution was carried out using a laboratory-made tumour lysis buffer (2 mg ml -1 collagenase type IV (Gibco), 2 U ml -1 dispase in Hanks' balanced salt solution (HBSS; StemCell Technologies), 10 U ml -1 DNase I (Sigma), serum-free RPMI medium) injected with a 20G needle and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. Prior to flow sorting, tumour suspensions were purified by negative selection with MojoSort magnetic bead separation (BioLegend) and biotin-conjugated anti-mouse antibodies to CD105 (MJ7/18, catalogue number 120403, lot number B266720, BioLegend). Skin (ears of mice were used) (Figs. 1g, 3c and Extended Data Figs. 1i, 4i-l) was finely minced using scissors and resuspended in RPMI1640 (Gibco) with 2.5 mg ml -1 collagenase XI (Sigma-Aldrich), 0.25 mg ml -1 hyaluronidase (Sigma-Aldrich), 0.1 mg ml -1 DNase (Sigma-Aldrich), 0.01 M HEPES (Sigma-Aldrich) and 10% FBS, followed by incubation in a shaking incubator for 1 h at 37 °C at 250 r.p.m. Following incubation, skin was subjected to mechanical separation and passed through a 70-μm filter. Similarly, muscles (quadriceps were used) were finely minced using scissors and then resuspended in 0.05% type II collagenase (ThermoFisher) in HBSS for 30 min at 37 °C under constant agitation. Islets were isolated as described 41 . Pimonidazole was visualized using anti-pimonidazole antibodies intracellularly (Hypoxyprobe, catalogue number HP7-100, lot number 5914) after 5 min of fixation first in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and then using the FoxP3 fix/perm kit (Biolegend).

Staining for expression markers was performed with anti-mouse specific antibodies obtained from the following companies: Biolegend: anti-CD4 (GK1.5, catalogue number 100412, lot number B184560, dilution 1:1,000), CD8 (53-6.7, catalogue number 100707, lot number B171971, dilution 1:1,000), CD39 (Duha59, catalogue number 143806, lot number B186014, dilution 1:500), CD44 (IM7, catalogue number 103032, lot number B267976, dilution 1:500), CD73 (TY/11.8, catalogue number 127215, lot number B260585, dilution 1:500), CD279 (PD-1, 29F.1A12, catalogue number 135221, lot number B194160, dilution 1:250), HAVcr-2 (TIM-3, RMT3-23, catalogue number 119705, lot number B224472, dilution 1:250), Helios (22F6, catalogue number 137220, lot number B155089, dilution 1:250), IFNγ (XMG1.2, catalogue number 505842, lot number B270630, dilution 1:250), Ki67 (16A8, catalogue number 652403, lot number B208445, dilution 1:250), CD304 (Nrp-1, 3E12, catalogue number 145212, lot number B175814, dilution 1:200), tumour necrosis factor (TNF)-α (MP6-XT22, catalogue number 506322, lot number B218553, dilution 1:500); BD Horizon: CD62L (MEL-14, catalogue number 564109, lot number 7341887, dilution 1:500); eBioscience: FoxP3 (FJK-16 s, catalogue number 53-5773-82, lot number 2011698, dilution 1:250). Intracellular staining was performed using the FoxP3 fix/perm buffer set (BioLegend) according to the manufacturer's protocol, and staining was performed overnight at 4 °C. Stained cells were analysed on a LSRFortessa (BD). Cell doublets were excluded by comparison of side-scatter and forward-scatter width to area. Flow-cytometry data and proliferation modelling were analysed with FlowJo version 10 software (Tree Star) and figures were produced in Prism version 8 (GraphPad).

## Glucose-uptake assay

Single-cell suspensions from tumours, lymph nodes and other tissues (roughly 1 million cells per millilitre) were placed in serum-free RPMI containing 40 μM 2NBDG or 0.4 μM glucoseCy5 for 25 min at 37 °C. GlucoseCy5, a Cy5-linked 1-amino-glucose tracer, was synthesized in collaboration with M. Bruchez 17 . For OT-II experiments, CD4 + T cells were isolated by negative selection using magnetic bead separation as described 4 , and then injected retroorbitally into C57BL/6J mice. Simultaneously, recipient mice were injected intraperitoneally with 1 × 10 6 vaccinia-OVA viral particles and collected 5 days later.

## Microsuppression and proliferation assays

In vitro microsuppression assays were performed as described 29 . In brief, T reg (CD4 + FoxP3 + ) cell populations were isolated by flow-assisted sorting from tumours or lymph nodes of C57BL/6JFoxp3 GFP.cre.ERT2 mice, C57BL/6JFoxp3 YFP.cre mice, or mice crossed to these strains. Responder cells (CD4 + Foxp3 T conv ) and antigen-presenting cells (CD4 -CD8 APCs) were isolated through flow-assisted sorting from the spleen of a C57BL/6JThy1 a mouse. T reg cells were cocultured for 72 h at 37 °C with APCs and CellTrace Violet (CTV, ThermoFisher Scientific)-labelled responder cells at ratios from 1:2 (T reg :responder) to 1:32 in complete RPMI medium with 1 μg ml -1 anti-CD3 antibodies. Proliferation assays were performed by labelling isolated T reg , T conv and CD8 T cells with CTV, and then activating them at one million cells per millilitre using anti-CD3/CD28 Dynabeads according to the manufacturer's protocol (ThermoFisher Scientific) and IL-2 in complete RPMI medium containing 0 mM to 10 mM lactic acid (Fisher Bioreagents). Proliferation of responding cell populations was modelled using FlowJo version 10 (Tree Star).

## RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen), and complementary DNA was transcribed using a high-capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit (Applied Biosystems), according to the manufacturer's protocol. Transcript levels were measured with SybrGreen (ThermoFisher Scientific), and PCR was carried out using primers specific to genes of interest, including: Slc16a1 , forward 5′-GCAGTGTTAGTCGGAGCC-3′, reverse 5′-GCGATCATTA CTGGACGGC-3′; Ldha , forward 5′-AGCTTCCATTTAAGGCCCCG-3′, reverse 5′-TCTTTTGAGACCGCTAGTGC-3′. Complementary DNA concentrations were normalized per samples relative to β-actin. All independent experiments were performed in technical triplicates.

## Cytokine-production assay

Cell suspensions were stimulated in complete R10 medium with 0.1 μg ml -1 PMA and 1 μg ml -1 ionomycin (Sigma-Aldrich) for roughly 20 h at 37 °C. Five hours before antibody staining, Golgi-Plug (BD Biosciences) was added to the samples. Cells were surface stained and then intracellularly stained using FoxP3 fix/perm buffer (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Unstimulated controls were used to determine gating strategies.

## Quantification of interstitial lactate

Tumours and spleens were collected and placed in empty 15 ml conical tubes. Tissues were cut up with scissors, wrapped with a 5-μm nylon filter paper (Sterlitech), and stuffed (filter down) into a 1.5 ml conical tube, making sure that the tissue did not touch the bottom. Tissues were centrifuged at 4,000 r.p.m. for 2 h. Interstitial fluid was assayed for l-lactate concentration using a colorimetric detection kit according to the manufacturer's protocol (Abcam).

## Isotopic flux analysis

Cell suspensions at 1 million cells per millilitre in complete RPMI medium were stimulated for 24 h at 37 °C with plate-bound anti-mouse CD3ε (10 μg for T reg cells, 3 μg for T conv cells, clone 145-2C11, catalogue number 100239, lot number B306297, Biolegend), soluble anti-mouse CD28 (2 μg ml -1 , clone 37.51, catalogue number 102115, lot number B283231, Biolegend) and IL-2 (1,000 U ml -1 for T reg cells, 50 U ml -1 for T conv cells). The activation medium was then exchanged for complete RPMI medium with 5 mM uniformly labelled 13 C-l-lactate (Sigma Aldrich) and IL-2. For some experiments, the PEPCK inhibitor 3MP (Cayman Chemical) was added at 250 μM. The medium was then titrated to pH 6.88 with 1 M HCl. Suspensions were pulsed for 25 h, washed twice with room-temperature PBS, and resuspended in ice-cold 80% methanol.

Metabolic quenching and extraction of polar metabolites were performed using ice-cold 80% methanol in water with 0.1% formic acid at a ratio of 400 μl per 100 μl. Deuterated (D4)-taurine and (D3)-lactate (Sigma-Aldrich) were added to the sample lysates as an internal standard to a final concentration of 100 μM. The supernatant was cleared of protein by centrifugation at 16,000 g . Cleared supernatant (5 μl) was subjected to online liquid chromatography with mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis.

Analyses were performed by untargeted liquid chromatography with high-resolution mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS). In brief, samples were injected via a Thermo Vanquish ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system and separated over a reverse-phase Phenomenex Kinetex C18 +  column (2.1 mm × 100 mm; particle size 1.7 μm) maintained at 40 °C. For the 20 min liquid-chromatography gradient, the mobile phase consisted of the following: solvent A (1.5 mM ammonium fluoride) and solvent B (100% acetonitrile). The gradient was the following: 0-12.0 min, 5% B to 1,000% B; 12.0-15.0 min hold at 100% B; 15.0-15.1 min, 100% to 5% B, 15.1-20.0 min, 5% B. The Q Exactive mass spectrometer was operated in polarity-switching mode, using both positive and negative ions, scanning in full MS mode (2 μscans) from 66.7 m / z to 1,000 m / z at 70,000 resolution with an automatic gain control (AGC) target of 3 × 10 -6 . Source ionization settings were spray voltages of 4.5 kV and 3.0 kV for positive and negative modes, respectively. Source gas parameters were 20 sheath gas, 10 auxiliary gas at 250 °C, and 4 sweep gas (arbitrary units). Calibration was performed before analysis using Pierce positive and negative ion calibration solutions (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Integrated peak areas were then extracted manually using Quan Browser (Thermo Fisher Xcalibur version 2.7). Fully untargeted analysis was completed by using the Thermo Compound Discoverer 3.0 software suite to extract and align molecular features. Graphs and statistical analyses (either t -test or ANOVA) were prepared with GraphPad Prism version 7.0 (GraphPad Software).

## In vivo tumour growth and therapy

Slc16a1 f/f Foxp3 cre or Foxp3 cre mice were inoculated with 1.5 × 10 5 B16-F10 cells in complete RPMI medium. To monitor tumour growth, tumour areas were determined three times weekly using digital calipers, and experiments were stopped once tumours reached 15 mm in any direction. For analysis of 3MP treatment, Foxp3 cre or Foxp3 cre.ERT2 mice were inoculated with 1.5 × 10 5 B16-F10 cells. Starting on day 10 or 12 after inoculation, mice were treated daily either with water or with 25 mg kg -1 3MP (Caymen Chemical) via intraperitoneal injection. On day 14, 1 h before being killed, mice were given a final dose of 25 mg kg -1 3MP or water. For tumour-growth experiments involving 3MP, treatment started on day 7 after tumour injection and continued daily with 25 mg kg -1 3MP or water intraperitoneally until tumours reached 15 mm in any direction.

For the inducible deletion of MCT1 ( Slc16a1 ), Slc16a1 f/f Foxp3 GFP.cre.ERT2 or Foxp3 GFP.cre.ERT2 mice were treated intraperitoneally with 1 mg of tamoxifen (T5648, Sigma) in corn oil (C8267, Sigma) from day -4 to day 0. On day 0, mice were inoculated intradermally with 2.5 × 10 5 B16-F10, MC38 or MEER cells in complete RPMI medium and given a dose of 1 mg tamoxifen. Tamoxifen treatment continued three times weekly until the conclusion of the experiment. On day 7, when tumours reached 1-10 mm 2 , mice were treated with 0.2 mg anti-PD-1 antibody or hamster IgG isotype control (Bio X Cell) three times weekly until the conclusion of the experiment.

## pHrodo Red assay for lactic-acid uptake

Cell suspensions from lymph nodes and day 14 B16-F10 tumours were loaded with pHrodo Red AM (ThermoFisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's protocol in 20 mM HEPES in PBS. Cells were surface stained for multicolour flow cytometry following the normal protocol in 20 mM HEPES/PBS. At the flow cytometer, lactic acid was spiked into each sample at a final concentration of 5 mM (pH roughly 6.7). Samples were read at 0 min, 10 min and 30 min after addition of lactic acid.

## Transfer colitis

Transfer colitis was performed as described 42 . In brief, 2NBDG hi and 2NBDG lo T reg cells from Foxp3-Ametrine reporter mice or T reg cells from Slc16a1 f/f Foxp3 cre or Foxp3 cre mice were isolated by flow-assisted sorting (CD4-APC + Foxp3-reporter + ). T conv cells (CD4 + CD25 ) from C57BL/6JThy1 a mice were isolated via flow-assisted sorting. Rag1 -/-mice were simultaneously given 2 × 10 4 sorted T reg cells and 1 × 10 5 Thy1.1 + Tconv cells via retroorbital injection. Mice were weighed once a week for seven weeks (to the end of the experiment). Sections of colons were fixed in 10% formalin and embedded in paraffin. Slides were stained with haematoxylin and eosin by the University of Pittsburgh Pathology Department, and histology scores were determined blindly using criteria described previously 42 .

## Tumour histology

After pulsing with pimonidazole, tumours were dissected and frozen at -80 °C in optimal cutting temperature compound (OCT; Tissue-Tek) and sectioned using a cryostat microtome. Tissue was fixed in histology-grade acetone (Fisher) at -20 °C, then rehydrated in staining buffer, stained with hypoxyprobe (Hypoxyprobe, catalogue number HP7-100Kit) and DAPI (Life Technologies), and mounted with ProLong Diamond antifade mountant (Life Technologies). Sections were imaged with an Olympus IX83 microscope and analysed with ImageJ and NIS-Elements imaging software.

## RNA sequencing

One thousand low-glucose- and high-glucose-consuming T reg cells were sorted directly into lysis buffer in a 96-well plate. Immediately after sorting, cDNA was generated using a SMART-seq HT kit (catalogue number 634456, Clontech) using 1,000 cells. The cDNA product was checked using a Tape Station D5000 from Agilent Technologies 2200 to ensure that cDNA was successfully generated. Library construction was carried out using a Nextrera XT kit (catalogue number 15031942) from Illumina, with 1 ng of cDNA in a total volume of 5 μl. Sequencing was done using a NextSeq 500 System and a high-output 75-cycles kit with run parameter 'paired read 150 cycles (2 × 75)'.

Sequencing reads were trimmed for adapters using Cutadapt 43 before being aligned to the Mus musculus reference genome (mm10) using the RNA-seq aligner HISAT2. Subread's featureCounts function was used for gene-level quantification, and results were normalized to transcripts per kilobase million (TPM). Using the raw quantification, differential genes were found using the R package DESeq2, with the following statistical cutoffs: q -value less than 0.05; log 2 -transformed fold change greater than 1.5.

## Statistical analysis

Where noted, data presented in the figures are means ± s.e.m. Multiple group comparisons in in vivo and ex vivo assays were accomplished by one- or two-way ANOVA. For single comparisons, unpaired two-tailed Student's t -test were used, unless otherwise denoted. In cases of non-Gaussian distribution, nonparametric t -tests were used. Flow cytometry data and proliferation modeling were analyzed with FlowJo v10 software (Tree Star) and figures were produced in Prism v8 (GraphPad).

Purity and yield of sorted cells was determined by post-sort staining on LSRII Fortessa or on the MoFlo Astrios sorter itself.

Lymphocytes were gated using FSC-Area and SCC-Area. Cell doublets were excluded by comparison of side-scatter and forward-scatter width to area. Viable cells were determined by zombie dye negative. See Extended Data Figure 1 for detailed gating strategy.
