## Deletion of PD-1 destabilizes the lineage identity and metabolic fitness of tumor-infiltrating regulatory T cells

## Received: 8 June 2021

Accepted: 27 October 2022

Published online: 28 December 2022

Myeong Joon Kim 1,2,8 , Kyungsoo Kim 2,3,8 , Hyo Jin Park 1 , Gil-Ran Kim 4,5 , Kyeong Hee Hong 1,2 , Ji Hoon Oh 1,2 , Jimin Son 1,2 , Dong Jin Park 1,2 , Dahae Kim 1,2 , Je-Min Choi 4,5,6,7 , Insuk Lee 2,3 &amp; Sang-Jun Ha 1,2

Regulatory T (T reg ) cells have an immunosuppressive function and highly express the immune checkpoint receptor PD-1 in the tumor microenvironment; however, the function of PD-1 in tumor-infiltrating (TI) Treg cells remains controversial. Here, we showed that conditional deletion of PD-1 in T reg cells delayed tumor progression. In Pdcd1 fl /fl Foxp3 eGFP-Cre-ERT2(+/-) mice, in which both PD-1-expressing and PD-1-deficient T reg cells coexisted in the same tissue environment, conditional deletion of PD-1 in T reg cells resulted in impairment of the proliferative and suppressive capacity of TI Treg cells. PD-1 antibody therapy reduced the TI T reg cell numbers, but did not directly restore the cytokine production of TI CD8 + T cells in TC-1 lung cancer. Single-cell analysis indicated that PD-1 signaling promoted lipid metabolism, proliferation and suppressive pathways in TI T reg cells. Treg cells expressing the transcription factor Foxp3 regulate immune homeostasis 1,2 . They accumulate in the tumor microenvironment (TME) in humans and mice 3 . TI T reg cells are highly suppressive and effectively inhibit antitumor immunity 3 . Hence, the accumulation of Treg cells and the T reg cell/CD8 + T cell ratio in the TME correlate with poor prognosis in cancer patients 3 . Immunotherapy is more effective and shows a higher response rate than other treatments for cancer 4 and can interfere with suppressive immune cells such as T reg cells, tumor-associated macrophages and myeloid-derived suppressive cells 5,6 . Given that inhibition of systemic T reg cells can trigger severe autoimmune diseases, immunotherapy should target only the TI T reg cells 3 . Targeting TI T reg cell-specific markers, such as the transcription factor NF-κB, co-receptor NRP1, epigenetic modulator EZH2 and the scavenger receptor CD36, which maintain TI T reg cell stability, is effective and has a synergistic effect with the PD-1 antibody immunotherapy in a murine melanoma model 4,7-9 . Therefore, an in-depth understanding of TI T reg cell stability and suppressive function can contribute to the improvement of cancer immunotherapy.

PD-1, an immune checkpoint receptor expressed on TI T cells, suppresses T cell function 10 . PD-1 inhibits T cell receptor (TCR) signaling by suppressing CD28 co-stimulatory signals in CD8 + T cells 11 . PD-1 is expressed in CD8 + T cells, CD4 + conventional T (T conv ) cells, T reg cells, tumor-associated macrophages and myeloid cells 12-14 and while the function of PD-1 in CD8 + T cells and CD4 + T conv cells is well documented,

Identifying the specific function of PD-1 in various immune cell subsets is important for better understanding the immunotherapeutic mechanism of PD-1 antibodies. It was reported that PD-1 expressed on T reg cells promotes the homeostasis and functionality of T reg cells 15-20 . As such, PD-1 expressed on T reg cells inhibits CD8 + T cell function by binding with PD-L1 expressed on CD8 + T cells during murine chronic viral infection 16 . PD-1 is crucial for T reg cell homeostasis during low-dose interleukin (IL)-2 therapy 15 . Moreover, PD-1 represses asparaginyl endopeptidase (AEP), which leads to Foxp3 degradation and maintains the expression of Foxp3 in induced T reg (iT reg ) cells 21 . Hence, PD-1 has an important role in maintaining T reg cell homeostasis and function; however, other studies have shown that PD-1 also suppresses T reg cell suppressive function, similar to CD8 + T cells 22-24 . PD-1 suppresses T reg cell function in patients with cancer who fail to respond to PD-1 antibody therapy 22 and PD-1 deficiency ameliorates disease progression in a mouse model of experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) 24 . Therefore, the function of PD-1 on T reg cells remains controversial. In the present study, we used mice with a T reg cell-specific conditional deletion of PD-1 and single-cell analysis to identify how PD-1 regulates the cell identity and function of TI T reg cells. Conditional deletion of PD-1 in TI T reg cells enhanced antitumor immunity by decreasing the stability and suppressive capacity of TI T reg cells and impaired Foxp3 stability. Single-cell analysis indicated that PD-1 expression in TI T reg cells increased clonal expansion of TI T reg cells and promoted pathways related to proliferation, suppression and lipid metabolism. Collectively, we suggest that PD-1 expression in TI T reg cells is important for the maintenance of T reg cell stability by promoting proliferation, expression of Foxp3 and lipid metabolism.

## Results

## Deletion of PD-1 in T reg cells controls chronic disease

We examined the expression of immune checkpoint receptors in various populations of CD3 + T cells from various murine tumor models. In TC-1 lung cancer tumor-bearing C57BL/6 mice at day 18 after intravenous inoculation, TI CD8 + T cells, CD4 + T conv cells and T reg cells expressed more PD-1, TIM3, TIGIT and LAG3 than their splenic counterparts (Fig. 1a,b). In addition, in both the spleen and TME, the expression of most immune checkpoint receptors (PD-1, TIM3, TIGIT and LAG3) was higher on T reg cells than on CD8 + T cells and CD4 + T conv cells (Fig. 1a,b). High immune checkpoint receptor expression in TI T reg cells was also observed in B16F10 melanoma tumor-bearing C57BL/6 mice at day 18 after intravenous inoculation and MC38 colon cancer tumor-bearing C57BL/6 mice at day 14 after subcutaneous inoculation (Fig. 1c-f).

In Pdcd1 fl/fl Foxp3 eGFP-Cre-ERT2(+/+) mice, which contain an eGFP-Cre-ERT2 cassette downstream of the Foxp3 promoter, Foxp3 + cells delete the loxP site of Pdcd1 locus after tamoxifen (TAM) administration and expressed eGFP (Extended Data Fig. 1a). In Pdcd1 fl/fl Foxp3 eGFP-Cre-ERT2(+/+) mice that received TAM intraperitoneally (hereafter PD-1 cKO ), but not in Pdcd1 fl/fl Foxp3 eGFP-Cre-ERT2(+/+) mice treated with vehicle (hereafter PD-1 Ctr ) for up to 24 d (Extended Data Fig. 1b) expression of PD-1 was ablated in Foxp3 + T reg cells (hereafter T reg cells, unless otherwise specified), but not in CD8 + T cells and CD4 + T conv cells (Extended Data Fig. 1c,d). At day 7 of TAM treatment, expression of PD-1 was lost in about 80 % of T reg cells in PD-1 cKO mice, whereas the expression of TIM3, TIGIT, LAG3 and CTLA4 was similar to that of T reg cells in PD-1 Ctr mice (Extended Data Fig. 1c,d). At day 12 of TAM treatment, the frequencies and absolute numbers of CD4 + T conv cells and T reg cells in the spleen and lung of PD-1 cKO mice were similar to those of PD-1 Ctr mice, while the frequency and absolute numbers of CD8 + T cells in PD-1 cKO mice were higher than those in PD-1 Ctr mice (Extended Data Fig. 1e,f). The frequency of PD-1, TIM3, TIGIT and LAG3 + CD8 + and CD4 + T conv cells was comparable in PD-1 Ctr and PD-1 cKO mice (Extended Data Fig. 1g), while approximately 20% of T reg cells were still PD-1 + in PD-1 cKO mice (Extended Data Fig. 1g). In addition, the expression of activation or proliferation markers (CD44, CD69, CD62L

and Ki67), T reg cell-associated markers (TIM3, TIGIT, LAG3, CTLA4, CD28, ICOS, CD25, CD103, CD39, CD73 and ST2) and transcription factors (Foxp3, Blimp1, GATA3, Eomoes, T-bet and TCF1) was similar in CD8 + and CD4 + T cells, including T reg cells, from the spleen and lung in PD-1 cKO mice compared to PD-1 Ctr mice at day 12 of TAM treatment (Extended Data Fig. 2a).

Because Cre-ERT2 is toxic to immune cells 25,26 , we tested whether the use of TAM-treated Foxp3 eGFP-Cre-ERT2 mice (hereafter Foxp3 cKO mice) affected our observations (Extended Data Fig. 2b). TC-1 tumor progression was comparable between Foxp3 cKO  and PD-1 Ctr mice (Extended Data Fig. 2c) and the frequencies of TI T reg cells and Ki67 + TI T reg cells were comparable between Foxp3 cKO and PD-1 Ctr mice (Extended Data Fig. 2d,e), indicating that the toxicity of Cre-ERT2 on Treg cells had no effect on tumor progression. Thus, we used PD-1 Ctr mice as a control group.

PD-1 cKO mice infected with lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus clone 13 (LCMV-Cl13) mice at day 12 post-TAM treatment showed lower virus titers in the serum, spleen, lung and kidney at day 8 and 15 after infection compared to PD-1 Ctr mice (Extended Data Fig. 3a,b). While the frequency of LCMV-specific H2D b GP33 + CD8 + T cells was similar between PD-1 Ctr and PD-1 cKO mice, the expression of PD-1 on these cells was significantly lower in PD-1 cKO mice (Extended Data Fig. 3c,d). Similarly, while the frequency of T reg cells was comparable between PD-1 Ctr and PD-1 cKO mice, the expression of Foxp3 was downregulated in PD-1 cKO Treg cells compared to PD-1 Ctr T reg cells (Extended Data Fig. 3e,f), indicating increased instability of PD-1 cKO T reg cells. PD-1 cKO T reg cells were less proliferative than PD-1 Ctr T reg cells, while apoptosis was similar at day 21 post-infection (Extended Data Fig. 3g,h). When stimulated ex vivo with LCMV-specific glycoprotein (GP) peptide, CD8 + T cells isolated from the spleen and lung of PD-1 cKO mice produced more interferon (IFN)-γ and tumor necrosis factor (TNF) than those from PD-1 Ctr mice at day 15 post-infection, while similar amounts of cytokine were detected at day 21 post-infection (Extended Data Fig. 3i-l). The ratio of IFN-γ + TNF + cells/IFN-γ + cells in CD8 + T cells, which is the capacity to secrete multiple cytokines, was higher in PD-1 cKO mice than PD-1 Ctr mice at both day 15 and 21 post-infection (Extended Data Fig. 3i-l). These results suggested that loss of PD-1 on T reg cells enhanced viral control by impairing the stability of T reg cells and by promoting the functionality of LCMV antigen-specific CD8 + T cells.

Next, we assessed tumor growth in PD-1 cKO and PD-1 Ctr mice. At day 20 post-injection of TC-1 and B16F10 tumor cells (on day 12 post-TAM treatment; Extended Data Fig. 4a), PD-1 cKO mice had delayed tumor growth and enhanced survival (Fig. 2a-d). The frequencies and absolute numbers of TI CD8 + and CD4 + T cells were comparable in PD-1 Ctr and PD-1 cKO mice (Fig. 2e,f), whereas the frequency and numbers of TI Treg cells were lower in PD-1 cKO mice compared to PD-1 Ctr mice (Fig. 2e,f). As such, the ratio of CD8 + T cells/T reg cells in the TME was increased in PD-1 cKO mice compared to PD-1 Ctr mice (Fig. 2f). These results indicated that loss of PD-1 on T reg cells enhanced viral control, reduced the TI T reg cell pool and delayed tumor growth.

Based on coexpression of annexin V and 7-aminoactinomycin (7-AAD), splenic and TI PD-1 cKO T reg cells were more susceptible to apoptosis and only TI PD-1 cKO T reg cells showed decreased proliferation compared to PD-1 Ctr T reg cells (Fig. 3a,b). Based on flow cytometric analysis, the expression of T reg cell markers (CTLA4, CD103 and CD25) and transcription factors (Foxp3, Blimp1) known to be associated with the stability or functionality of T reg cells 1,27-29 was downregulated in TI, but not in spleen, PD-1 cKO compared to PD-1 Ctr T reg cells (Fig. 3c-f and Extended Data Fig. 4b,c). ## PD-1 maintains T reg cell stability during chronic antigen stimulation

PD-1 was reported to inhibit the suppressive activity of T reg cells in an EAE model 24 . PD-1 cKO mice immunized with myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG) peptide in complete Freund's adjuvant emulsion at day 12 after TAM treatment showed a lower disease score assessed by a scoring method as in ref. 24 (Extended Data Fig. 9a) and less body weight loss (Extended Data Fig. 9b) than PD-1 Ctr mice on day 10 after immunization. Based on a different disease scoring method 31 , PD-1 cKO mice had more severe disease than PD-1 Ctr mice at day 7-9, but disease severity did not progress in PD-1 cKO mice (Extended Data Fig. 9a). The frequency and cell number of central nervous system (CNS)-infiltrating CD4 + T cells, including T reg cells, was lower in PD-1 cKO compared to PD-1 Ctr mice (Extended Data Fig. 9c) and the expression of Foxp3 in T reg cells was lower in PD-1 cKO T reg cells compared to that in PD-1 Ctr T reg cells (Extended Data Fig. 9d). The frequency of MOG-specific CD4 + T conv cells in the spinal cord was higher in PD-1 cKO compared to PD-1 Ctr mice, but the absolute cell number of MOG-specific CD4 + T conv cells was comparable between PD-1 Ctr and PD-1 cKO mice (Extended Data Fig. 9e). The frequency of IFN-γ + IL-17A + CD4 + T conv cells was higher in PD-1 cKO mice (Extended Data Fig. 9f), suggesting CD4 + T conv cells were more activated in PD-1 cKO mice than in PD-1 Ctr mice and, as such, that PD-1 cKO T reg cells were less stable than PD-1 Ctr T reg cells during EAE.

Next, we investigated the cause of the discrepancy between a lower EAE disease score and a lower T reg cell stability in PD-1 cKO mice. ## PD-1 therapy reduces the TI T reg cell pool

To test whether PD-1 blockade could influence the stability of TI T reg cells, PD-1 Ctr and PD-1 cKO mice were injected intraperitoneally with PD-1 antibody every 3 d for 12 d, starting at day 1 after inoculation of TC-1 cells (conducted at day 12 after TAM treatment). PD-1 antibody treatment delayed tumor growth and enhanced the survival of both PD-1 Ctr and PD-1 cKO mice compared to PD-1 Ctr and PD-1 cKO mice treated with isotype, respectively (Fig. 6a,b). At day 14 after TC-1 cell injection, the frequency of TI T reg cells was lower in PD-1 antibody-treated compared to isotype-treated PD-1 Ctr mice (Fig. 6c), whereas the frequency of TI T reg cells was similar in PD-1 antibody-treated and isotype-treated PD-1 cKO mice (Fig. 6c). TI T reg cells from PD-1 antibody-treated PD-1 Ctr mice were less proliferative and expressed less Foxp3 compared to their counterparts in isotype-treated PD-1 Ctr mice, while apoptosis was similar (Fig. 6d). Ex vivo PMA + Iono stimulation induced comparable IFN-γ and TNF production in CD8 + T cells isolated from the tumors of isotype- or PD-1 antibody-treated PD-1 Ctr mice at day 14 after TC-1 cell inoculation (Fig. 6e), suggesting that PD-1 antibody blockade did not restore the functionality of TI CD8 + T cells in the TC-1 lung cancer model. The frequency of TI T reg cells and expression of Foxp3 and Ki67 in TI T reg cells were similar in PD-1 antibody- or isotype-treated PD-1 cKO mice (Fig. 6c,d), whereas IFN-γ and TNF production was higher in TI CD8 + T cells in PD-1 antibody-treated compared to isotype-treated PD-1 cKO mice (Fig. 6e). PD-1 antibody treatment also delayed tumor growth in PD-1 Ctr mice, but not in PD-1 cKO mice, injected with B16F10 tumor cells (Fig. 6f), indicating that PD-1 antibody therapy was dependent on the presence of PD-1-expressing T reg cells. These results suggested that PD-1 antibody therapy reduced the TI Treg cell pool and reinvigorated the functionality of TI CD8 + T cells.

## Discussion

In the present study, we showed that PD-1 expression in T reg cells was required for the maintenance of T reg cell stability and functionality. PD-1 cKO mice were resistant to tumor progression and had reduced number of TI Treg cells, which showed impaired stability and less suppressive capacity. PD-1 KO T reg cells were more unstable and less functional than PD-1 WT T reg cells in the same TME. Foxp3 tracing experiments indicated expression of PD-1 was important for the stability of Foxp3 expression in TI T reg cells. Single-cell analysis revealed that PD-1 expression regulated pathway related to proliferation, suppression and lipid metabolism in TI T reg cells, thereby promoting their clonal expansion and suppressive capacity. Collectively, these observations suggested that T reg cell-specific deletion of PD-1 enhanced antitumor immunity by reducing the stability, suppressive capacity and metabolic fitness of TI T reg cells.

In the TME, the expression of immune checkpoint receptors (PD-1, TIM3, TIGIT and LAG3) was higher on T reg cells than CD8 + T cells, suggesting that immune checkpoint receptors expression on TI T reg cells could be as important for tumor progression as their expression on TI CD8 + T cells. PD-1 antibody treatment partially recapitulated the effects of PD-1 deletion in T reg cells. PD-1 expression was important for the activation and proliferation of naive T reg cells and PD-1 deletion was effective in controlling tumor growth if it occurred before tumor inoculation. Because PD-1 antibody might bind to PD-1 expressed by both naive and activated T reg cells, the blocking activity of PD-1 antibodies might be more effective in naive, PD-1 lo T reg cells than in activated, PD-1 hi T reg cells, suggesting that the assessment of PD-1 expression on TI T reg cells at various stages of tumor evolution may inform on the suppressive potential of TI T reg cells and can be informative for PD-1 antibody therapy. Additionally, antibody-mediated PD-1 blocking does not completely exclude basal PD-1 signaling in the presence of PD-L1 ligation. In terms of our observation that the effect of PD-1 antibody treatment was effective in reducing TI T reg cell number, rather than the functional restoration of TI CD8 + T cells in the TC-1 lung cancer model, the effect of PD-1 blockade on T reg cells might also be applicable to patients with cancer who have prevalent exhausted CD8 + T cells, because fully exhausted T cells, unlike stem-like and/or less exhausted T cells, do not restore their cytotoxicity and proliferation by PD-1 antibody treatment 41,42 . Given that the efficacy of PD-1 antibody therapy is quite variable in clinical practice, the timing of the initial treatment and the differentiation status of T cells, including T reg cells, could be determinants of the clinical efficacy of PD-1 antibody therapy for patients with cancer.

We found that Foxp3, which was reported to regulate the suppressive function of T reg cells by repressing glycolysis and enhancing oxidative phosphorylation 36,43,44 , was downregulated in TI PD-1 KO T reg cells compared to TI PD-1 WT T reg cells, suggesting that PD-1 signaling controlled T reg cell metabolism and suppressive function by maintaining Foxp3 expression in the TME. In an adoptive transfer model in which lymphocytes injected into irradiated mice underwent lymphopenia-induced homeostatic proliferation, PD-1 signaling was reported to repress the suppressive function of T reg cells and PD-1 blockade in T reg cells promoted accelerated B16F10 tumor progression. During lymphopenia-induced homeostatic proliferation, certain T cell clones proliferate selectively 45,46 , resulting in a restricted T cell repertoire. It is thus possible that the observation reported above 22 were characteristic of certain T cell clones. A different study reported no difference in the frequency and proliferation of TI PD-1 + T reg cells in non-small cell lung cancer patients receiving PD-1 antibody therapy with and without hyperprogression 47 and suggested that hyperprogression was not related to TI T reg cell activity, but rather to the pretreatment frequency of CD39 + CD8 + T cells. In mice with EAE, PD-1 expression was reported to inhibit the immunosuppressive function of T reg cells 24 . It is possible that inflammation in the lung prevented the licensed T cells from infiltrating the CNS. We did not evaluate the role of PD-1 in T reg cells in other autoimmune disease models. Moreover, environmental factors, cytokines, metabolic features and other factors can regulate the status of T reg cells in a disease-specific manner. Therefore, the context-dependent function of PD-1 in T reg cells still needs to be investigated.

Two studies suggesting a functionally suppressive role of PD-1 in T reg cells used Pdcd1 fl/fl Foxp3 YFP-Cre mice, in which the PD-1 gene is deleted in T reg cells during thymic development. PD-1 is expressed on thymocytes and regulates Treg cell development in the thymus and peripheral tissues 48-50 . ## Housing, data randomization, blinding and exclusion

For in vivo experiments, PD-1 Ctr and PD-1 cKO mice were never cohoused to prevent TAM cross-contamination. All mice used in experiments were socially housed under a 12-h light-dark cycle, with ambient temperature of 20-26 °C and humidity of 30-70%. For in vivo experiments, age-matched littermates of Foxp3 cKO , Pdcd1 fl/fl Foxp3 eGFP-Cre-ERT2(+/+) , PD-1 fl/+ Rosa 26tdTomato , PD-1 cKO Rosa26 tdTomato mice were randomly allocated. Experimental mice (Foxp3 cKO , PD-1 Ctr , PD-1 cKO , PD-1 fl/+ Rosa 26tdTomato and PD-1 cKO Rosa26 tdTomato mice) were 6-8 weeks old of either sex. For using PD-1 Cre+/mice, age-matched female PD-1 Cre+/mice were randomly allocated. No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample sizes but our sample sizes are similar to those reported in previous publications 16,19,51 . No blinded experiments were conducted in this study, because this study was an observational study. No data points or mice were excluded for analysis in this study.

## Tamoxifen treatment

TAM was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and suspended (100 mg) in 0.5 ml of 100% ethanol and 9.5 ml of corn oil purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Pdcd1 fl/fl Foxp3 eGFP-Cre-ERT2(+/+) mice  were  treated with 1 mg of vehicle or TAM every 5 d by intraperitoneal injection. Foxp3 eGFP-Cre-ERT2 , PD-1 Cre+/, PD-1 fl/+ Rosa26 tdTomato and PD-1 cKO Rosa26 tdTomato mice were treated with 1 mg TAM every 5 d by intraperitoneal injection. TAM-treated mice were injected with murine cancer cells at day 12 after treatment.

## LCMV infection

LCMV-clone 13 (Cl13), a variant derived from an LCMV Armstrong (Arm) AC1371 carrier mouse, was obtained from R. Ahmed (Emory Vaccine Cancer). Vehicle- and TAM-treated 6-week-old Pdcd1 fl/fl Foxp3 eGFP-Cre-ERT2(+/+) mice were infected with 2 × 10 6 plaque-forming units (p.f.u.) of LCMV-Cl13 diluted in serum-free RPMI medium by intravenous injection.

For virus titration, three to five drops of blood were collected from the retro-orbital sinus at the indicated time points and the serum was immediately stored at -70 °C. Viral titers from serum were determined by the plaque assay on Vero cells as below 52 . Briefly, 3.5 × 10 5 Vero cells

(obtained from ATCC in 2009) were plated in 35-mm wells in six-well dishes (Costar) and incubated at 37 °C. When the cell monolayers were confluent, the medium was removed and known amounts of virus in serum (in a total volume of 200 μl) were added to the cells. After adsorption for 1 h at 37 °C, the cells were overlaid with 4 ml of 1% agarose (SeaKem GTG agarose; Lonza) in Medium 199 (Gibco Laboratories) supplemented with 10% FBS, antibiotics (Gibco Laboratories) and l-glutamine (Gibco Laboratories). The plates were incubated for 4 d at 37 °C and then overlaid with 3 ml of 1% agarose in medium 199 containing 1% neutral red (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Plaques were counted the following day.

To  detect  degranulation  and  intracellular  cytokines  in T cells, splenocytes and lymphocytes from lungs were incubated with LCMV-glycoprotein peptide (GP) pools, including 0.2 μg ml -1 GP33-41 , GP276-286 , GP 118-125 , GP 92-101 and GP70-77 peptide, in the presence of GolgiPlug/GolgiStop at 37 °C for 5 h, followed by intracellular cytokine staining using anti-IFN-γ (dilution 1:100) and anti-TNF (dilution 1:100). Intracellular staining was performed after surface staining using a BD Cytofix/Cytoperm fixation/permeabilization kit (BD Biosciences) according to the manufacturer's instructions. All stained samples were analyzed on a CytoFLEX LX instrument (Beckman Coulter) using FlowJo software (TreeStar).

## Cell isolation, antibodies and staining

Lymphocytes from peripheral blood were collected from the interphase of collected blood and histopaque-1077 Hybri-Max (Sigma-Aldrich) layers after density-gradient centrifugation. Lymphocytes were isolated from the digested spleen after removing red blood cells by ACK lysis buffer (Gibco Laboratories). The lungs were perfused with ice-cold PBS before removal for lymphocyte isolation. The lungs were digested with a type IV collagenase and DNase I mixture for 30 min at 37 °C shaking incubator. Lymphocytes were isolated from the digested samples after removing red blood cells by ACK lysis buffer (Gibco Laboratories). For phenotypic analysis of lymphocytes, single-cell suspensions were stained with the fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies. To measure the proliferation capability ex vivo, lymphocytes from the spleen and lungs were stained with a Ki67 (dilution 1:20) antibody (B56). For analysis of apoptosis ex vivo, lymphocytes from the spleen and lungs were incubated with annexin V and 7-AAD (BD Biosciences). The LIVE/DEAD fixable dead cell stain kit (Invitrogen) was used to remove the dead cells in most staining procedures, except for apoptosis staining.

To detect intracellular cytokines in T cells, splenocytes and TI lymphocytes were incubated with PMA (50 ng ml -1 ) and Iono (500 ng ml -1 ) in the presence of GolgiPlug/GolgiStop at 37°C for 5 h, followed by intracellular cytokine staining using anti-IL-2 (dilution 1:100), anti-IFN-γ (dilution 1:100) and anti-TNF (dilution 1:100). After incubation, cells were washed with FACS buffer for surface and intracellular staining. ## Tumor experiments

B16F10 murine melanoma, TC-1 lung adenocarcinoma, MC38 colon adenocarcinoma cell lines were obtained from ATCC in 2010 and cultured in complete DMEM or RPMI supplemented with 10% FBS, penicillin (100 U ml -1 ) and streptomycin (100 mg ml -1 ). Tumor cells were tested yearly for mycoplasma contamination using the e-Myco Mycoplasma PCR Detection kit (iNtRON Biotechnology). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in humidified air with 5% CO2 and used for in vivo experiments after 2-3 passages. For tumor metastasis models, mice were intravenously injected with B16F10 (5 × 10 5 cells per mouse) or TC-1 (5 × 10 5 cells per mouse) tumor cells via the tail vein. The mice were killed, and their lungs were collected at day 20 after tumor cell injection. The total number of visible nodules on one lung lobe was counted. For survival experiments, tumor-injected mice were monitored until death or until the appearance of moribund symptoms, such as lethargy, weight loss and a hunched back. For subcutaneous tumor models, B16F10 (1.25 × 10 5 cells per mouse), TC-1 (5 × 10 5 cells per mouse) or MC38 (5 × 10 5 cells per mouse) tumor cells resuspended in 100 μl of PBS were injected subcutaneously in the shaved left flank. Tumor growth was monitored every 2-3 d by measuring the tumor size using a caliper and tumor volume was calculated as the length × width 2 × 0.5. In the subcutaneous model, mice were killed when the tumors reached 15 mm in diameter.

## Experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis model

For the EAE model, we used the EAE induction kit (Hooke Laboratories, EK-2110) according to the manufacturer's instructions. vehicle- and TAM- treated 10-12-week-old female Pdcd1 fl/fl Foxp3 eGFP-Cre-ERT2(+/+) mice were subcutaneously immunized with 200 μg MOG35-55 peptide in complete Freund's adjuvant emulsion. At 1 and 24 h after immunization, the Pdcd1 fl/fl Foxp3 eGFP-Cre-ERT2(+/+) mice were intraperitoneally treated with 150 ng of pertussis toxin. Mice were monitored daily for signs of clinical disease and scored as follows: 1, limp tail; 2, weak gait; 3, hind limb paralysis; 4, hind limb and forelimb paralysis; and 5, morebund 24 or 0.5, partially limp tail; 1, completely limp tail; 1.5, limp tail and waddling gait; 2, paralysis of one hind limb; 2.5, paralysis of one hind limb and partial paralysis of the other hind limp; 3, paralysis of both hind limbs; 3.5, ascending paralysis; 4, paralysis of trunk; 4.5, moribund; and 5, dead 31 .

Pdcd1 fl/fl Foxp3 eGFP-Cre-ERT2(+/+) mice were killed 19 d after immunization. Spinal cord was extracted from Pdcd1 fl/fl Foxp3 eGFP-Cre-ERT2(+/+) mice and digested with a type IV collagenase and DNase I for 20 min at 37 °C. Lymphocytes were collected from the interphase of the 30% and 70% Percoll (Sigma-Aldrich) layers after density-gradient centrifugation.

To detect intracellular cytokines in T cells, lymphocytes from the lung and spinal cord were incubated with PMA (50 ng ml -1 ) and Iono (500 ng ml -1 ) in the presence of GolgiPlug/GolgiStop at 37°C for 5 h, followed by intracellular cytokine staining using anti-IL-17A (dilution 1:100) and anti-IFN-γ (dilution 1:100). Intracellular staining was performed after surface staining using a Foxp3/transcription factor staining buffer set (eBioscience) according to the manufacturer's instructions. All stained samples were analyzed on a CytoFLEX LX instrument (Beckman Coulter) using FlowJo software (TreeStar).

For histology analysis, lungs were trimmed and placed in labeled cassettes and fixed in formalin for 24 h for further embedding in molten paraffin wax. Paraffin sections at a thickness of 3-5 μm were stained with H&amp;E according to standard procedures. Images were taken and exported on a Lionheart FX automated microscope.

## Cell sorting

For scRNA-seq, lungs from TC-1 tumor-bearing PD-1 Cre+/mice were digested with a type IV collagenase and DNase I mixture. Digested lung samples were stained with anti-CD4 and the LIVE/DEAD fixable dead cell stain kit for sorting of live CD4 + T cells. For the ex vivo suppression assay, lungs from TC-1 tumor-bearing Pdcd1 fl/fl Foxp3 eGFP-Cre-ERT2(+/+) mice treated with 1 mg of TAM digested with type IV collagenase and DNase I were stained with CD4, CD25 and PD-1 antibodies and a LIVE/ DEAD fixable dead cell stain kit was used to sort live CD4 + CD25 + PD-1 + and CD4 + CD25 + PD-1 T reg cells. The stained cells were sorted using a FACSAria II instrument (BD Bioscience).

## Ex vivo suppression assay

For the ex vivo suppression assay, SP CD8 + T cells were enriched naive 6-week-old female C57BL/6 mice by using the MagniSort Mouse CD8 T cell enrichment kit (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Enriched SP CD8 + T cells were labeled with 5 μM CellTrace Violet (Invitrogen). A total of 1 × 10 5 SP CD8 + T cells were cultured with sorted CD4 + CD25 + PD-1 + or CD4 + CD25 + PD-1 -T reg cells at the indicated T reg cell to CD8 + T cell ratios in the presence of 1 × 10 5 Dynabeads mouse T-activator CD3/CD28 (Invitrogen) for 72 h. GolgiPlug/GolgiStop was added to detect intracellular cytokines in CTV-labeled CD8 + T cells during the final 5 h.

## In vitro T reg cell proliferation assay

For the in vitro T reg cell proliferation assay, SP T reg cells were enriched from PD-1 Cre+/mice by using a CD4 + CD25 + Regulatory T Cell Isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotec) according to the manufacturer's instructions. SP CD11c + dendritic cells were enriched from naive 6-week-old female C57BL/6 mice by CD11c + microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec), followed by negative selection using Thy1.2 + and CD19 + microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec) according to the manufacturer's instructions. A total of 5 × 10 4 SP Treg cells were cultured with 5 × 10 4 SP dendritic cells in the presence of anti-CD3 antibodies (3 μg ml -1 ) for 3 d. Before co-culture, SP T reg cells were preincubated with isotype or PD-1 antibody (10 μg ml -1 ) for 20 min at a 37 °C incubator.

## In vivo suppression assay

For the in vivo suppression assay, SP CD4 + T conv cells (naive 6-week-old female Ly5.1 + mice) and PD-1 Ctr or PD-1 cKO T reg cells (6-week-old Ly5.2 + PD-1 Ctr or PD-1 cKO mice) were enriched by using a CD4 + CD25 + Regulatory T Cell Isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotec) according to the manufacturer's instructions. A total of 4 × 10 5 naive CD4 + T conv cells (Ly5.1 + ) were transferred intravenously alone or together with 1 × 10 5 PD-1 Ctr or PD-1 cKO T reg cells (Ly5.2 + ) into Rag2 -/-recipients. Recipient mice were monitored and weighed every 2-3 d after transfer for signs of disease, such as weight loss.

## In vivo blockade of PD-1 signaling

To block PD-1 signaling in vivo, a total 1 mg of the PD-1-blocking antibody (clone RMP1-14; Bio-X-Cell) or rat IgG2a (clone 2A3; Bio-X-Cell; isotype control) was administered to TC-1 tumor-bearing PD-1 Ctr , PD-1 cKO mice or PD-1 Cre+/mice every 3 d five times on the first day after tumor cell injection. At 14 d after tumor cell injection, mice were killed, and lymphocytes collected from the spleen and tumors were analyzed.

## Single-cell RNA and VDJ sequencing

For scRNA-seq and scVDJ-seq, we sorted 1 × 10 6 TI CD4 + T cells from isotype- or PD-1 antibody-treated PD-1 Cre+/mice. Single-cell sequencing libraries were prepared by using the Chromium 10x Genomics platform with a VDJ Enrichment kit and a 5′ Library &amp; Gel Bead kit (10x Genomics, PN-1000005, PN-1000006) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The single-cell suspensions of live-sorted TI CD4 + T cells were diluted in nuclease-free water and then combined with Single-Cell 5′ Gel Beads, a master mix and Partitioning Oil on Chromium Chip A, found in the aforementioned kit. RNA transcripts were uniquely barcoded and reverse-transcribed within nanoliter-scale droplets containing uniquely barcoded beads. Next, 10x barcoded full-length cDNA was pooled and enriched via PCR. For the VDJ enriched library, the enriched cDNA pool was amplified using primers specific to the TCR. For the 5′ Gene Expression library, the cDNA pool was subjected to an end repair process, A-tailing, adaptor ligation and sample index PCR. The libraries for both scRNA-seq and scVDJ-seq were sequenced using the HiSeqX platform (Illumina).

We used the Seurat R package to process the data 53,54 . Two different batches were integrated into one dataset using the integration process described in Seurat 54 . After examining the JackStrawPlot 55 , we determined the number of input dimensionalities to be used for downstream dimension reduction analysis. TI CD4 + T cells were clustered using the Louvain algorithm described in Seurat 56 . Each cluster was named based on its expression of canonical marker genes. The expression of genes in UMAP spaces was visualized using the FeaturePlot function in Seurat.

To further analyze the T reg cells from the experiment, clusters C3 and C4 were selected for downstream analysis. We reanalyzed those clusters using a different number of dimensionalities for downstream dimension reduction analysis. The distribution of each origin was visualized by the contour plot. To estimate the differences in transcriptional profile between T reg cell subsets, we calculated the Euclidean distance between centroids of two different clusters of cells. For each replicate experiment, the measured Euclidean distance was divided by the Euclidean distance between isotype PD-1 KO T reg cells and PD-1 antibody PD-1 KO Treg cells  to  obtain  the  relative  centroid  distances.  For  differe ntially expressed gene analysis, we used the FindMarkers function in the Seurat package.

For VDJ-seq data, clonotypes assigned to multiple cells were named 'expanded clonotypes' and those assigned to a single cell were named 'unique clonotypes'. Approximately 90.4% of the T reg cells analyzed by VDJ-seq were successfully clonotyped. Among them, 64.7%, 15.1% and 14.0% of the cells were expanded over two times, three times and four times, respectively. The inequality of clonotypes was measured by Gini index 57 .

GSVA was performed by using the GSVA function in R 58 . The log transformed expression value from the Seurat object was used to calculate the GSVA scores. The gene sets for GSVA were obtained from our previous study 51 and MSigDB 39,40 .

GSEA was performed by using the function GSEA from the package clusterProfiler 39,40,59 . The ranked gene list was imported from the differentially expressed genes obtained above. The gene sets for GSEA were obtained from MSigDB and a previous study 4 .

A heat map was drawn using the pheatmap function from the package pheatmap 60 . The scaled expression value was imported from the Seurat object. The relationships between genes were visualized by hierarchical clustering.

## Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Prism software v.7.00 (GraphPad). Significant differences between two groups were analyzed by the two-tailed unpaired Student's t -test. For subcutaneous tumor models, two-ANOVA and Sidak's multiple comparisons test were used. KaplanMeier survival curves were analyzed using the Mantel-Cox log-rank test with a 95% CI. P &lt; 0.05 was considered statistically significant. A Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for scRNA-seq and scVDJ-seq data.
